\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Defining and scoping home IoT}
\label{sec:related-def}
Our operational definition of a home IoT device follows the residential-deployment
framing of Balta-Ozkan et al.~\cite{balta2013} and the home-based IoT security
evaluation of Alrawi et al.~\cite{alrawi2019}, whose four-component (device, mobile app,
cloud, communication) deployment model and hub-in/router-out scoping our criteria
inherit. Prior taxonomies classify home IoT by \emph{function} rather than keyword
alone: Bugeja et al.~\cite{bugeja2018} build an empirical taxonomy of 1{,}193 commercial
smart-home products organized by functional area (security, climate, appliances,
entertainment); Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2019} analyze devices on real home networks and
define device classes that explicitly separate computers, phones, and routers from home
IoT; and FakhrHosseini et al.~\cite{fakhrhosseini2025} survey expert perspectives on
home-automation taxonomy. On the standards side, NIST's consumer-IoT reviews and
baselines~\cite{nistir8267,nistir8259a,nistir8425} enumerate the same retail categories
(cameras, doorbells, plugs, thermostats, televisions) and define an IoT device as a
system with at least one transducer and one network interface, supporting our
connectivity criterion. Hammi et al.~\cite{hammi2022} frame smart-home vulnerabilities
around physical risks to residents---burglary, occupancy inference, and device
hijacking---matching our consumer-ownership criterion.

\subsection{Consumer versus commercial and industrial scope}
\label{sec:related-scope}
A recurring boundary in our review is that enterprise, industrial, and commercial
devices are out of scope even when they share a brand or keyword with consumer products.
This distinction is well established: Xenofontos et al.~\cite{xenofontos2022} present an
attack taxonomy separating consumer, commercial, and industrial IoT with distinct threat
models; Wurm et al.~\cite{wurm2016} compare consumer home-automation devices against
industrial smart-meter and operational-technology equipment; and Alladi et
al.~\cite{alladi2020} and Ugwuanyi and Irvine~\cite{ugwuanyi2021} reinforce a
consumer-grade scope through case studies and consumer/industrial threat-landscape
analysis~\cite{alahmadi2023}. On the networking axis, Stanford's minimum-security
standard~\cite{stanford_iot} explicitly excludes routers, switches, and access points
from its IoT definition, supporting our decision to treat pure-transport gear as
threat-model context rather than as study subjects.

\subsection{Device-category studies}
\label{sec:related-cat}
Individual categories in our set are recognized as distinct device classes in prior
work. For cameras, doorbells, and baby monitors, Dahmen et al.~\cite{dahmen2017} and
NIST~\cite{nistir8267} treat video doorbells as separate from standalone cameras, while
Valente et al.~\cite{valente2019} and Albrecht and McIntyre~\cite{albrecht2015} analyze
dedicated baby monitors separately from enterprise surveillance, and
Bugeja et al.~\cite{bugeja2018cameras} perform a global assessment of internet-exposed smart
cameras. Locks, sensors, and alarms appear as core subjects in the smart-home platform
analyses of Fernandes et al.~\cite{fernandes2016} and the security/safety taxonomies of
Bugeja et al.~\cite{bugeja2018} and Hammi et al.~\cite{hammi2022}. Climate devices
(thermostats, HVAC) are covered by Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2019}, and bedside sleep
monitors---distinct from wrist wearables---by Apthorpe et
al.~\cite{apthorpe2017}. Plugs, lighting, and appliances are dissected
experimentally by Notra et al.~\cite{notra2014}, Loi et al.~\cite{loi2017}, Davis et
al.~\cite{davis2020}, and Ali and Awad~\cite{ali2018}. Hubs, voice assistants,
and streaming surfaces are treated as home-control device classes by Alrawi et
al.~\cite{alrawi2019}, Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2019}, Vetrivel et al.~\cite{vetrivel2023},
the voice-control taxonomy~\cite{hewitt2022}, and Fernandes et
al.~\cite{fernandes2016}. Energy, pet, and outdoor categories draw on residential
EV-charging security~\cite{johnson2022,johnson2022sandia} and systematic reviews of smart-home
device types~\cite{builgil2023}. Because companion apps are in scope for our review, we
also draw on the mobile-companion-app vulnerability
literature~\cite{mauro2019,neupane2022,schmidt2023}. Broader threat-landscape context
is provided by the Mirai botnet analysis~\cite{antonakakis2017}, smart-home security
surveys~\cite{yang2022,vardakis2024}, and the OWASP IoT project~\cite{owasp}.

\subsection{NVD-based and AI-assisted vulnerability characterization}
\label{sec:related-method}
Methodologically, our pipeline is closest to recent work that mines NVD for IoT
vulnerabilities and confronts the same false-positive problem. Rezaei et
al.~\cite{rezaei2026} (LLIoT) combine a written IoT definition, keyword filtering, a
triple-expert 300-CVE ground truth, and an LLM labeler over the full 2013--2024 CVE set,
reporting LLM agreement around 95\%---directly motivating our blind multi-model review,
though we differ by using three blind AI reviewers plus human adjudication rather than a
single production LLM. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025} filter ${\sim}198$k CVEs to IoT via
keyword search plus manual verification and analyze high-risk vulnerabilities, and Singla
et al.~\cite{singla2023} label CPE \texttt{vendor:product} pairs into
compute/IoT/networking bins with keyword-plus-human labeling---closest to our CPE-centric
expansion step. FirmRec~\cite{xiao2024} likewise seeds from NVD using mixed vendor and
keyword terms, but for firmware rather than a home-scoped dual method. We differ from all
of these in restricting scope to home IoT across 24 categories, running two independent
discovery methods (vendor and keyword) reconciled per category, and following the
CWE-888 profiling approach of prior empirical vulnerability studies~\cite{ref-mcsoftware}.

\section{Our Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes our methodology. We decide what a home IoT device is
(Section~\ref{sec:method-scope}) and define it as an OWL ontology
(Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}). Then we collect candidate CVEs from the
NVD~\cite{ref-nvd} against a snapshot using two search methods: device/keyword
and vendor/brand based. This is done for each of our 24 categories and split
into three directions (intersection, device/keyword only, vendor/brand only). A
CPE-based expansion step deepens coverage of CVEs already found. Automated
passes go through the snapshot to compile terms to add to the search methods,
and each candidate is confirmed by humans before being added. A false-positive
review is run on every candidate, where terms that produce false positives are
pruned, and work to estimate what terms aren't reached through
capture-recapture estimate. Finally, each in-scope CVE has its CWE
classification and CVSS data analyzed by grouping the CWE into the CWE-888
Software Fault Pattern clusters~\cite{ref-mcsoftware} and comparing the CVSS
scores. The study is observational in nature over publicly available and
reputable NVD data.

\paragraph*{The framework stage by stage} Collection works as a combination of
ten stages, numbered in the order that a CVE encounters them, with analysis
being the eleventh stage that takes the output of the first ten, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. The list below exists to make dependencies between
stages clear and provide a brief overview of said stages before they are
expanded upon further.

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{1}\ \textbf{Keyword search.} Generic device-type
phrases, one set per category (208 phrases in total), which name what a product
is rather than who makes it. This list deliberately excludes brands, protocols,
and firmware strings, so that the two text-based search methods stay separate.

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{2}\ \textbf{Vendor search.} Manufacturer and
brand strings for each category, with 1{,}564 pairs of (category, term) over
1{,}540 different brands.

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{3}\ \textbf{NVD snapshot.} Both lists are matched
by an engine against one offline snapshot of the NVD (373{,}518 records as of
2026-08-05), against the CVE description as well as the CPE strings associated
with it, on whole-word boundaries. Running both search methods through the
same engine and the same corpus makes a gap between them attributable to terms
rather than staleness of data.

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{4}\ \textbf{Reconciliation into disjoint
directions.} For each category, the resulting sets are combined by set
operations into three parts: vendor-only, keyword-only, and the intersection.
Initially, we blindly trusted the intersection, but in a correction to our
original design (Section~\ref{sec:method-collect}) we now review it.

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{5}\ \textbf{Blind AI review.} Every candidate row
in every direction is judged independently by 3 AI reviewers:
\circulo{5.1}~Claude, \circulo{5.2}~Codex, and \circulo{5.3}~Gemini.
Blindness is structural so that each reviewer fills in its own judgement
without context from the other judgements (Section~\ref{sec:method-review}).

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{6}\ \textbf{Decision making.} The three judgement
columns are merged into a single verdict per row, and rows that do not settle
confidently are flagged for human review (Section~\ref{sec:method-review}).

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{7}\ \textbf{Human resolution.} The flagged rows
are adjudicated blindly by two human reviewers, and a row settles only when
both humans agree on a verdict. Their verdict then returns to the store
(Section~\ref{sec:method-review}).

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{8}\ \textbf{Home IoT benchmark.} All AI judgements,
human verdicts, and final labels persist in one store keyed by (category, CVE).
This judgement allows the pipeline to be re-runnable, since regenerating the
candidate sets only reviews new rows and never repeats settled work
(Section~\ref{sec:method-review}).

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{9}\ \textbf{Refinement and discovery.} Settled
judgements close the loop back. \circulo{9.1}~CPE expansion runs from the
home IoT benchmark back to the snapshot: for every confirmed-\texttt{Yes} CVE
it pulls every other CVE NVD attributes to the same CPE, and re-enters the
review chain at stage~5, deepening coverage of devices already in the corpus.
A per-term precision pass, \circulo{9.2}~keyword and vendor refinement, joins
each judgement back to the term that pulled the CVE in and flags terms which
have a high false-positive rate, so removal is directly based on measured data
(Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}). \circulo{9.3}~Keyword discovery harvests
device-type n-grams from confirmed matches that the phrase list is missing, and
\circulo{9.4}~CPE vendor discovery harvests brands from the NVD's vendor field
and device nouns from its product field. Mined candidates are never accepted
automatically, and everything is confirmed by hand before entering a list.

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{10}\ \textbf{Recall estimation.} Since review only
measures precision, vendor and keyword searches are treated as two captures
occasions of the same CVE population, which bounds what the terms never
reached (Section~\ref{sec:method-recall}).

\smallskip\noindent\circulo{11}\ \textbf{CVE analysis.} The confirmed CVEs are
characterized by CWE classes and CVSS scores
(Sections~\ref{sec:method-extract} and~\ref{sec:method-cwe888}).
\smallskip

One element of the framework is not a stage in this sequence. The
\textbf{category list} sits upstream of everything: generated from the
ontology (Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}), it partitions every term list,
result set, review direction, and reported distribution. Because so much of
the pipeline is partitioned by category, we froze category membership before
collection while keeping the term lists open.

\subsection{Scope: what counts as a home IoT device}
\label{sec:method-scope}

We define a home IoT device as an internet-connected sensor, appliance, or
embedded system deployed in a residential environment for the purpose of
monitoring, automation, or control, owned and maintained by non-expert consumers
without dedicated IT security oversight~\cite{balta2013,alrawi2019}. A device is
in scope only if it satisfies all five criteria below.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Connectivity} --- it communicates over a standard network protocol
(TCP/IP, MQTT, CoAP, Zigbee, BLE)

\item \emph{Device class} --- it is a special-purpose sensor, appliance, or
embedded system, not a general-purpose IT (PC, phone, tablet, server, game
console)

\item \emph{Deployment context} --- it is intended for a private residence, not
primarily for enterprise or industrial use

\item \emph{Function} --- it qualifies if either
  \begin{enumerate}
  \item its primary purpose is to monitor, automate, or control the home
  environment or its systems (climate, security, access, lighting, appliances,
  presence); or

  \item it serves as a home-control surface or hub for \emph{other} home IoT
  devices --- discovering, controlling, or displaying their state (a
  Matter/Thread controller or border router, a voice assistant, a
  camera/sensor feed surface). General-purpose computing and pure media playback
  with no control role do not qualify.
  \end{enumerate}

\item \emph{Security context} --- it is owned and maintained by consumers with
no professional security administration
\end{enumerate}

A sixth, operational (not definitional) criterion scopes what we can
\emph{study}: the device must have a CPE-identifiable footprint in the NVD and be
subject to CVE disclosure. Almost every networked product satisfies connectivity,
so criteria~2 and~4 --- class and function --- do the actual discriminating: a
device that merely interoperates with home IoT is not itself home IoT.

This is why entertainment hardware is in scope only when it doubles as a
home-control surface under criterion~4(b): streaming TVs, sticks, and boxes
qualify because their platform runs a Matter/Thread controller, an assistant, or
surfaces camera/doorbell feeds, as do smart speakers and
displays~\cite{kumar2019,fernandes2016}. Game consoles and VR/AR headsets do not,
remaining general-purpose IT excluded on criteria~2 and~4. The same logic applies
to networking: what matters is whether a device \emph{controls} other home IoT
devices, not whether it carries their traffic. IoT hubs, bridges, and
Matter/Thread/Zigbee controllers are in scope; pure transport gear (routers,
modems, switches) is not, and appears only as untrusted threat-model
context~\cite{alrawi2019,stanford_iot}.

The study analyzes 24 categories: cameras, doorbells, baby monitors, door locks,
alarms, sensors, thermostats, air conditioners, fans, air purifiers, smart plugs,
lighting, refrigerators, robot vacuums, other appliances, hubs, smart speakers,
sleep trackers, shades, EV charging, home power, garden, pet, and streaming
devices. Two device types are separate categories only if a consumer would call
them different products \emph{and} their brand sets differ meaningfully;
otherwise we merge them. Smart displays and soundbars therefore fold into
\texttt{smartspeakers}, and blinds, curtains, and shutters fold into
\texttt{shades}, while cameras, doorbells, and baby monitors stay distinct
despite overlapping hardware, since their brand sets differ. Game consoles, VR/AR
headsets, and pure-transport networking gear satisfy connectivity but fail class
or function, and stay excluded. \texttt{sleeptracker} stays on the list but is
dominated by wrist wearables (out by criterion~3) and contributes nothing to
analysis pending a rebuild. We froze category membership before large-scale
collection, so a later change to one category only re-runs that category's
chain; the per-category term lists remain open throughout.

\subsection{Formalizing the scope as an ontology}
\label{sec:method-ontology}

We also publish the five criteria as a machine-readable OWL ontology, for three
reasons. First, it makes scope decisions auditable: every in-scope and excluded
type carries an explicit value for all five criteria, criteria~1--5 compose into
one equivalence axiom, and an OWL reasoner --- not prose --- decides membership.
Reproducing our published rulings is a weak test on its own, since most rulings
are in-scope categories confirming they are in scope, which the axiom satisfies
by construction. The stronger check deletes each of the five conjuncts from the
axiom in turn and requires the reasoner to then admit a device it should exclude;
it does, on one defined-but-excluded boundary type per criterion --- a
non-networked detector (1), a tablet control panel (2), a commercial HVAC
controller (3), pure-transport networking gear (4), and a professionally
monitored alarm service (5) --- so all five criteria are load-bearing (reported
as a construct-validity check in Section~\ref{sec:threats-construct}).

Second, the ontology records the route of admission, not just the verdict: of
the 1{,}738 confirmed category assignments, 1{,}624 (93.4\%) enter through
criterion~4(a) and 114 (6.6\%) through 4(b), so the boundary that attracts the
most methodological objection --- home-control surfaces such as streaming TVs
and smart speakers --- governs a small minority of the corpus. Third, it defines
a broader tier of 13 folding categories over the 24 leaves, used when a leaf
category is too small to interpret on its own (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}). The
ontology generates \texttt{categories.csv}, the same file that supplies every
reviewer's scope note, so the formal model and the artifact the pipeline runs on
cannot drift apart. The file holds only classes and scope notes: no CVE is
classified by the ontology, and no reviewer sees information its prose scope note
did not already contain, so judgments derived from the ontology cannot
self-confirm the term-precision scores of Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}.

Once review settles, we export the analyzed data as an instance graph over this
vocabulary (80{,}072 triples: 1{,}676 vulnerabilities, 1{,}738 category
assignments, 4{,}707 device products, 292 vendors, 21 CWE-888 classes). A gate
re-derives every count --- per-category totals, folding totals, CWE-888
attributions --- from the judgment store and CWE mapping
(Sections~\ref{sec:rq1}--\ref{sec:rq2}) and checks it against the export, so the
published graph cannot drift from the files the analysis actually runs on. Two of
the 24 categories have no confirmed CVEs and appear in the graph as empty
classes.

Beyond the five membership criteria, the ontology also carries \emph{descriptive
facets} (placement, cloud dependence, whether a device actuates something
physical, and the consequence if it does) used to group categories for analysis.
None appears in the membership axiom, so none can move a scope ruling. Their
evidentiary status is deliberately modest: all 496 facet assertions are marked
\texttt{Estimated} --- hand-assigned from domain knowledge with no external
source --- and the build gate prints that mix on every run. We subject the facet
vocabulary to three independent checks, any of which can disqualify a facet
(results in Section~\ref{sec:threats-reliability}). \emph{Validity}: facets are
asserted per category but joined onto CVE rows, so we sampled devices within each
category (272 devices, 10 categories with enough CVE mass to sample, 120
category--facet cells) and measured what share of a cell's devices actually take
its asserted value, on the same 0.80/0.60 bands used for agreement below. Failing
cells are withheld from the analysis in code, not by a note to the reader; a
second withholding route covers cells where two reviewers split along a
device-subfamily line in categories too small to sample. The remaining 14
categories stay unmeasured rather than assumed sound, and roughly a fifth of
confirmed assignments carry no device CPE at all and so cannot be
product-sampled at any budget. \emph{Reliability}: three annotators
independently labelled a shared subsample (471 facet--item judgments across
35--40 devices per facet), and we report Fleiss' $\kappa$~\cite{fleiss1971}; only
4 of 12 facets clear the conventional $\kappa \geq 0.60$ bar~\cite{landis1977}.
\emph{Sourcing}: a per-category pass attached an external source to each facet
value where possible, settling 185 cells (94 citations, 91 reasoned judgments
with no source, none vendor-documented); because a citation is only evidence
about \emph{this} corpus if it covers it, we score each cited source by the
share of the category's confirmed CVEs its vendors account for, and demote a
cell below that floor to an unsourced judgment. Taken together, these checks are
why we treat facets as organizing structure only: they group categories and
generate hypotheses, and no finding in this paper uses one as its grounding
variable.

Two further artifacts are verified against pinned manifests. The first aligns
our classes to established smart-home vocabularies: SAREF core~\cite{saref} and
its S4BLDG~\cite{saref4bldg}, S4ENER, S4WEAR, S4AGRI, S4AUTO, S4CITY, and S4EHAW
extensions~\cite{saref4ext} (eight SAREF vocabularies), plus W3C
SSN/SOSA~\cite{ssn2017}. Alignment uses only subclass (\texttt{rdfs:subClassOf})
and SKOS match relations (broad, close, related), never class equivalence, since
our classes are narrower than the external ones they map to --- our camera class
is residential consumer IP cameras, while \texttt{saref:Sensor} is any sensor.
Every external IRI is checked against a pinned 435-IRI manifest, each entry
recorded with a retrieval date and content hash and, where the vocabulary
publishes one, a version, so a reader can reconstruct the exact document rather
than its current version. Two of the eight SAREF vocabularies (S4AUTO, S4CITY)
yielded nothing in scope, which we treat as a result rather than an omission;
Section~\ref{sec:threats-construct} reports what the alignment did find. The
second artifact records, per category, the literature that motivates its
inclusion, separating studies that evaluate the device type by name from those
whose method covers it without naming it --- collapsing that distinction would
overstate how well grounded the category set is. Of the 24 categories, 13 (54\%)
have a study evaluating them directly, 10 are supported only by methodology, and
\texttt{streaming} has neither; weighted by confirmed CVEs, 14.6\% of the
analysis set sits on categories no study in the manifest examines directly.

\subsection{Collecting candidate CVEs (stages 1--4)}
\label{sec:method-collect}

A CVE is a publicly known security vulnerability reported in NVD~\cite{ref-nvd}
and attributed to specific software, firmware, or hardware. We ran every search
offline against one fixed snapshot: the per-year NVD JSON feeds (2000--2026)
pulled from the NVD 2.0 API on 2026-08-05, merged into 373{,}518 unique CVE
records. Freezing the corpus this way makes it reproducible and citeable as of
that date, following established practice~\cite{bhandari2021}, and removes
data-lag as a difference between the two search methods; collection is therefore
a point-in-time picture, not a date range. We searched for home IoT CVEs with two
complementary text methods --- stage~1 (keyword) and stage~2 (vendor), run in
parallel against the same snapshot --- and augmented them with a CPE-based
expansion step that works from NVD's own attribution rather than from text. Terms
the two hand-compiled lists are missing are mined from the snapshot itself, but
that pass reads settled judgments as its evidence, so we describe it with its
pruning counterpart in Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}, downstream of review.

\paragraph{Vendor/brand search (stage 2)} For each category we compiled the
manufacturers and brands that make that type of device, by surveying major
smart-home ecosystems (Google Nest/Home, Apple Home, Amazon Alexa, comparable hub
platforms) for certified device brands, supplemented by targeted per-category web
searches. Where a bare brand name overlaps unrelated products, we qualified it
with a product word (e.g.\ ``carrier infinity'', not ``carrier''). Every term is
matched against both the CVE description and its CPE strings, on whole-word
boundaries with a trailing plural allowed --- blocking substring hits such as
\texttt{nvr} in \texttt{nvram} or \texttt{evse} in \texttt{prevsell}, since CPE's
field-separating punctuation also counts as a boundary. One concession is
CPE-specific: a space in a multi-word term also matches an underscore, so
\texttt{hunter douglas} reaches \texttt{hunter\_douglas:\ldots}; without it, a
multi-word brand could only ever match through the description, silently losing
its CPE matches. Each output row records which term(s) matched it, feeding the
per-term precision analysis of Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}; the list also
absorbs vetted candidates from the discovery passes described there. The final
list holds 1{,}564 category--term pairs over 1{,}540 distinct brands. Because
brand names frequently overlap unrelated products, this method returns more
candidates but is more prone to false positives.

\paragraph{Device-keyword search (stage 1)} In parallel, we searched for generic
device-type phrases (``ip camera'', ``video doorbell'', ``smart lock''),
restricted to device-type language with no brands, protocols, or firmware names
--- brand discovery is the vendor search's job. This runs through the same
engine, the same snapshot, the same description-plus-CPE match, and the same
whole-word rule as the vendor search, emitting the same output schema. The list
is far smaller, at 208 phrases, since it names device types rather than
enumerating manufacturers. The two searches therefore differ only in their
terms, which is what makes their outputs directly comparable.

\paragraph{Reconciliation (stage 4)} For each category we split the two result
sets by set operations into three disjoint parts that partition their union:
\emph{vendor-only} (vendor CVEs the keyword search missed) and
\emph{keyword-only} (keyword CVEs the vendor list missed) surface gaps in each
list, and the \emph{intersection} holds CVEs both methods found. We review all
three, which was not the original design: we initially treated the intersection
as high-precision by construction and reviewed only the two differences. An
audit showed that assumption fails --- only 70.2\% of intersection rows are true
matches overall, concentrated exactly where a category's generic device
vocabulary collides with a professional product line (67.0\% in
\texttt{cameras}, 15.8\% in \texttt{smartspeakers}). Method agreement measures
that two term lists overlap, not that a device is in scope. Reviewing all three
directions also feeds confirmed true matches back into whichever term list was
missing them.

\paragraph{CPE expansion} As a further, text-independent discovery step, we
expand the set of CVEs NVD attributes to devices already confirmed in scope.
Seeding only from confirmed (\texttt{Yes}) rows, we take each device's
\texttt{vendor:product} CPE, restricted to \texttt{part} = hardware or operating
system (dropping vendor-only and shared application/library CPEs), and scan the
snapshot for every other CVE NVD attributes to that same product, subtracting
what the two text searches already found. Three constraints keep this honest: it
may seed only from a settled true match, never an unreviewed CVE; it seeds from
\texttt{vendor:product}, not from a bare vendor, so a confirmed smart plug
expands to that product line and not to everything its manufacturer makes; and
general-purpose computing platforms are denied outright even though they are
operating-system CPEs, so a vendor that attributes one CVE across an entire OS
family cannot drag the whole desktop/mobile corpus in behind one confirmed
device. The resulting candidates flow back through the same review and are never
accepted automatically.

Yield is strongly category-dependent, and the aggregate figure is misleading
before the scope exclusion below is applied. The step returned 2{,}068
candidates, of which reviewers confirmed 1{,}960 --- but 1{,}897 of those are
Apple TV expansions later removed by the tvOS exclusion, so precision on the 171
candidates that reach the analysis set is 36.8\% (63 of 171). Within that
remainder the split is stark: all 38 \texttt{alarms} candidates were confirmed,
tracing to one prolific product whose terse CVE text named no brand our terms
would catch, while \texttt{cameras} confirmed 15 of 67. The method faithfully
propagates whatever its seeds imply, making it a high-value densifier for
products NVD describes by CPE rather than by name, and a source of noise
wherever a confirmed seed sits in a crowded product namespace.

\paragraph{Comparability} An earlier iteration ran the keyword search against
the live NVD API, matching description text only, while the vendor search ran
offline over a snapshot matching description plus CPE. That asymmetry made
apparent ``gaps'' between the methods partly an artifact of data lag and match
surface rather than a genuine difference in what each found. We eliminated it by
running both term lists through the same offline engine, against the same
snapshot, matching description plus CPE under the same whole-word rule, so the
only remaining difference between the searches is the terms themselves.

\paragraph{Snapshot vintage} Pinning the snapshot makes the two searches
comparable; it does not mean the corpus never moves. The invariant is that both
lists always run against the same vintage, which a re-download preserves at a new
date so long as both are re-run. We did this once, replacing a 2026-06-25
snapshot (360{,}981 records) with the 2026-08-05 snapshot (373{,}518) used
throughout this paper, and rebuilt both searches and every downstream set
against it. Because settled judgments persist in a store keyed by (category,
CVE), the refresh cost only 111 genuinely new review rows out of 5{,}122; the
previous vintage was retained rather than overwritten, letting us measure what
NVD had changed on already-analyzed records before cutting over
(Section~\ref{sec:method-extract}).

Because the vendor and keyword searches are purely text-based, generic brand
names and terms inevitably produce false positives --- a brand string that also
names an unrelated commercial product, or marketing copy that merely mentions a
device category. We classify each candidate CVE in the difference set
independently as a true match or a false positive.

Every row is judged independently and blind by three AI reviewers, and the rows
they leave unsettled are blind-adjudicated by two human researchers (Aarav Verma
and Suhani Assija).
% DOUBLE-BLIND: if the venue requires anonymized submission, replace the two names above
% with "two of the authors" and drop the acknowledgement of individual reviewers.
The AI reviewers are Anthropic's Claude, via Claude Code (judgments collected
2026-06-01--07-02); OpenAI's ChatGPT Codex (06-15--06-30); and Google's
\texttt{gemma-4-31b-it}, via the Gemini API
(06-16--07-01)\stub[optional: add the exact Claude and Codex model version
strings if they can be recovered]. Each reviewer judges every row in a single
pass, with no self-consistency sampling, and one model is held fixed for a
reviewer's whole column. We record collection dates rather than internal build
identifiers for the two interactively accessed reviewers, since the identifiers
were not captured at run time --- a reproducibility limitation we note rather
than resolve. Blindness is structural: each reviewer works from its own copy of
the data, containing only the raw fields and its own empty judgment columns, so
no reviewer can see another's answer. All three apply one shared rubric and one
shared per-category scope note, judging only from a row's \texttt{description}
and \texttt{cpe\_strings}; one scope note per category is what makes agreement
between reviewers mean agreement under a common scope, not three readings of a
one-word label.

Each reviewer assigns a \emph{Judgment} (\texttt{Yes}/\texttt{No}/\texttt{Maybe}),
a \emph{Confidence} (\texttt{High}/\texttt{Low}), and a short \emph{Reasoning},
required for \texttt{Low} and \texttt{Maybe} rows; \texttt{Maybe} always pairs
with \texttt{Low}. We reserve Low confidence for the harder calls: a device
plausibly residential but primarily commercial, a description naming the
category while the CPE points at enterprise hardware, or a CVE in a
software/protocol layer shared between home and non-home contexts. A missing CPE
does not by itself force a \texttt{Maybe} --- CPE is frequently absent on recent
(2024--2026) CVEs from NVD enrichment lag, so an unambiguous description is
judged on its content. The rubric also fixes two recurring calls: a device's own
companion app and vendor cloud API count as part of the device, since the
app--device--cloud coupling is a defining property of a consumer IoT deployment
rather than grounds to exclude it~\cite{alrawi2019} (the converse does not hold:
an unrelated app that merely controls home IoT still fails class and function);
and an app-layer vulnerability spanning several of a vendor's product lines is
accepted for every named line, keeping per-category counts additive rather than
dependent on review order.

Two of the three AI reviewers (Claude Code, ChatGPT Codex) run manually, and
Gemini is scripted against its API. All three judge the combined review set for
a category --- vendor-only, keyword-only, intersection, and CPE-expansion rows
together, with a \texttt{Difference Type} field recording each row's origin ---
and their judgments are merged. We flag a row for human adjudication when both
Claude and Codex report Low confidence, or when the three judgments are not
unanimous; Figure~\ref{fig:review-confidence} is the empirical case for keying
the flag this way --- unanimity is 91.3\% when both report High confidence and
falls to 4.1--36.6\% when either is Low. We relax this in one case: a lone
Gemini dissent does not flag a row where Claude and Codex both agree at High
confidence, since we validated that relaxation against a fully worked human
queue, where humans sided with the Claude--Codex pair on 293 of 294 such rows
(99.7\%; only 95.5\% at any confidence, which is why High confidence is required
on both). Such rows settle as \texttt{strong-consensus} and stay in a
high-confidence audit pool with a recorded Gemini-dissent flag, rather than
joining the human queue. Gemini's judgment counts toward unanimity; its
confidence is recorded but excluded from the flag, since it is the weaker third
vote.

Humans then adjudicate only the flagged rows. Each CVE's judgments collapse into
one final label (\texttt{ai-consensus} for unflagged rows, \texttt{human} for
adjudicated ones). The human stage mirrors the AI stage in requiring agreement: a
flagged row settles only when both human reviewers agree on a non-\texttt{Maybe}
verdict; one verdict, a conflict, or a \texttt{Maybe} stays outstanding for the
next round, so no final label rests on a single human reading. Human verdicts are
sticky --- unaffected by later flag-rule changes or queue regenerations,
superseded only by a fresh agreeing pair. At analysis time nothing was
outstanding and no \texttt{Maybe} survived. All settled judgments persist in a
store keyed by (category, CVE); regenerating a difference set after a term-list
change restores prior judgments automatically and reviews only genuinely new
rows.

excluded rows as exclusions rather than deleting them or reversing their
judgments, keeping the review outcome and the scope decision separable in the
published data.

\subsection{Closing the loop: pruning and discovery (stage 9)}
\label{sec:method-precision}

Both term lists are refined between passes, using settled judgments as evidence:
pruning removes terms shown to generate noise, and discovery proposes terms the
lists never had.

\paragraph{Pruning by per-term precision} The matched-term attribution recorded
at collection lets us join each settled judgment back to the term(s) that pulled
its CVE in, and compute a per-term precision --- the fraction of a term's judged
difference-set matches confirmed true. We flag as prune candidates any term at or
below $0.10$ precision over at least 5 judged rows. Precision is computed per
(term, category, search method), since a string can be registered for more than
one category or on both lists: of 426 such combinations with at least one judged
match (398 distinct strings), 20 crossed the threshold, over 19 distinct terms.
Most are vendor terms naming professional or enterprise product lines (e.g.\
\texttt{synology surveillance station}, \texttt{geutebruck}, \texttt{digital
watchdog} under cameras, each at $0.0$ over 7--25 rows), plus broad consumer
brands that dominate their category's false positives (\texttt{d-link} under
cameras, $0.044$ over 1{,}802 rows). Five are keyword rather than brand terms
(\texttt{smart display}, \texttt{home hub}, \texttt{evse}, \texttt{charge
point}, \texttt{weather station}) --- umbrella phrases broad enough to match
marketing copy rather than a device. \texttt{smart display} is registered on
both lists and is flagged twice, which is why 20 flagged combinations span 19
terms; the same threshold pruning both lists is deliberate, since the keyword
list is not immune to over-breadth just because it avoids brand names. Because
these labels cover only the difference set, this is a prioritized prune list,
not a global precision over all of a term's matches.

\paragraph{Automated term discovery} A hand-compiled list is incomplete in ways
its author cannot introspect --- the brands one does not know about are exactly
the ones never searched. We mine the snapshot for missing terms on three
separate text surfaces. The first mines the CPE \emph{vendor} field for
manufacturers co-occurring with confirmed devices, weighting vendors on
confirmed true matches above vendors on merely keyword-matched, unjudged CVEs.
The second mines confirmed true-match \emph{descriptions} for device-type
n-grams, scored both for how strongly they discriminate confirmed from rejected
rows and for how many CVEs they would newly add, measured through the pipeline's
own matcher. The third mines the CPE \emph{product} field for single device
nouns --- precise inside a product name even though too generic for a free-text
keyword (\texttt{camera} matches half of NVD in text, but
\texttt{\ldots\_camera\_firmware} is a camera), and the only surface on which
several of our vendors were reachable at all. Each pass discards candidates
already covered by an existing term, scores survivors for the new CVEs they
would add, and flags known hazards --- mega-vendors whose footprint dwarfs their
in-scope products, dictionary words, terms with an established false-positive
record --- as triage, not a filter. None of the three passes writes to a term
list directly: each emits a candidate file, and accepting a term is a human
judgment against the flags and yield estimate, which is also why discovery is
not chained into the automated pipeline. The one vetting pass to date accepted
41 mined terms (17 brand, 24 phrase); the product-noun scan currently runs 47
tokens across 19 categories, and accepted tokens enter as ordinary brand terms,
since the underscore concession of Section~\ref{sec:method-collect} lets them
reach CPE strings directly.

The two passes are deliberately asymmetric: discovery proposes terms a
hand-compiled list is missing and requires a human to accept each one, since
accepting a brand is a scope judgment; pruning removes terms already shown to
generate noise, using labels the review produced, so its evidence is measured
rather than predicted. Both write only candidate lists, and both run between
pipeline passes rather than inside one, so a term list never changes mid-review.

\subsection{Estimating what the searches missed (stage 10)}
\label{sec:method-recall}

Review (Section~\ref{sec:method-review}) establishes precision but says nothing
about recall: a device-relevant CVE naming none of our terms never enters the
corpus at all. We estimate recall by treating the vendor and keyword searches as
two independent capture occasions of the same CVE population and applying the
Chapman-corrected Lincoln--Petersen estimator~\cite{chapman1951}, which needs no
additional labelling. Pooled across categories, combined search-stage recall is
0.603 (95\% CI 0.556--0.648), over 4{,}849 observed CVEs against an estimated
population of 8{,}046. This is an \emph{upper} bound: the two searches are
positively dependent rather than independent --- a CVE naming a brand often also
names the device type --- and such dependence biases the estimated population
downward and recall upward.

Because that dependence is the estimate's main weakness, we also fit a
three-source variant that does not have to assume it away, adding a third
capture occasion --- every CVE NVD attributes to a confirmed-in-scope device CPE
--- and selecting among Poisson log-linear models~\cite{fienberg1972}, with and
without pairwise interaction terms, by AIC~\cite{akaike1974}, letting the data
estimate vendor--keyword dependence rather than assuming independence. Of the 22
categories that support any estimate, 21 support a three-source fit; their
intervals are wide enough that we read per-category figures as indicative rather
than precise, and the third capture set can only reach products that already
appeared in one of the two searches. Three structural cases carry no estimate at
all: five categories are \emph{degenerate} (one search's results a subset of the
other's, so recapture carries no information and the estimator returns an
artifactual 1.0 --- excluded from the pooled figure); one category has no
in-scope seed and so forms no third set; and two have an empty term list on one
side, so no recapture is possible. We treat recall as an order-of-magnitude
coverage check rather than a measurement, and return to its limitations in
Section~\ref{sec:threats-external}.

\subsection{CWE and CVSS data extraction}
\label{sec:method-extract}

Each CVE is typically attributed to one or more CWEs; some lack enough
information for NVD to map one, recorded as \texttt{NVD-CWE-noinfo} or
\texttt{NVD-CWE-Other}. Of the 1{,}676 confirmed CVEs in the analysis set, 118
(7.0\%) carry no usable CWE mapping (our merged year-feed snapshot collapses
\texttt{noinfo}, \texttt{Other}, and absent values into one empty field, so we
report them together). We keep these in the CVE-level analysis
(Sections~\ref{sec:rq1}--\ref{sec:rq2}) but exclude them from the CWE analysis;
the remaining 1{,}558 CVEs carry 155 unique CWE identifiers before CWE-888
mapping.

A second, independent gap affects CPE: 193 of the 1{,}676 CVEs (11.5\%) carry no
CPE at all, the population reviewers had to classify from description text alone
(Section~\ref{sec:method-review}), and the ceiling on CPE expansion. We report
both gaps as standing limitations rather than artifacts a later snapshot would
clear: comparing against the preceding vintage six weeks earlier, NVD had
backfilled a CWE on none of the 118 CWE-less rows, a CPE on 3 of the then-196
CPE-less rows, changed no base score, re-scored no v2.0 record under v3.x, and
rejected nothing. All three real changes were 2026 CVEs NVD was analyzing for the
first time, so the comparison measures first analysis, not reanalysis: churn on
already-analyzed records is close to nil, and neither gap is closing at a rate
that would change these figures.

For each CVE we also extracted CVSS data. Scores range from 0 to 10, derived
from exploitability and impact metrics~\cite{ref-cvss}; severity bands are None
(0), Low (0.1--3.9), Medium (4.0--6.9), High (7.0--8.9), and Critical (9.0--10).
The corpus mixes CVSS v2, v3.x, and v4.0; where a CVE carries more than one, we
select one in the fixed preference order v3.1, v3.0, v4.0, v2.0. Placing v4.0
below both v3 versions is deliberate --- mixing base-score versions within one
distribution is a confound, so we keep the bulk of the corpus on v3.x and fall
back to v4.0 only where no v3 metric exists, recovering CVEs NVD scored under
v4.0 alone; v2.0, the oldest and least comparable standard, is last. Under this
rule the 1{,}676 confirmed CVEs resolve to 1{,}211 v3.1, 330 v3.0, 54 v4.0-only,
and 81 v2.0-only scores, and every one of the 1{,}676 carries both a base score
and a vector string, so none is dropped from the severity analysis. That
completeness came from a correction on our side, not NVD: 57 CVEs had previously
extracted as unscored, of which 54 were scored only under v4.0 and missed by a
reader that never inspected the v4.0 block (the other 3 were CVEs NVD analyzed
for the first time in the interval); vector strings were not extracted at all
before that pass. A silent parser gap of this kind is indistinguishable from
missing upstream data unless the two vintages are compared directly, which is
the argument for retaining the superseded snapshot. We did not perform a
component-level extraction of the vulnerable component from each description; a
breakdown mirroring the transportation study's Table~IV is left to future work
(Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).

\subsection{Mapping individual CWEs to the CWE-888 view}
\label{sec:method-cwe888}

CWE is a community-developed taxonomy of common weaknesses maintained by
MITRE~\cite{ref-cwe}; several views organize the hierarchy for different
purposes. We use the CWE-888 Software Fault Patterns view~\cite{ref-cwe888} for
two reasons: it was used by a prior study~\cite{ref-mcsoftware}, keeping our
results directly comparable, and it centers on fault categorization with wider
coverage than alternatives such as CWE-699 Software
Development~\cite{ref-cwe699}. We pin the catalog to version 4.12 (29 June 2023)
for reproducibility, and focus on the view's 23 primary clusters (``primary
vulnerability classes''); 21 receive at least one attribution from our corpus,
and 2 receive none but stay in the mapping.

When an identified CWE is itself in the CWE-888 view, we map it directly to its
primary class; otherwise we map its parent CWEs instead, so a CWE whose parents
fall under two different classes counts toward both. This over-counting is
bounded: of 1{,}613 (category, CVE) assignments carrying at least one CWE
(1{,}571 resolving to at least one primary class, 42 to none), 76 (4.7\%) have a
single CWE whose parents span two classes, and a further 220 (13.6\%) list two or
more distinct CWEs, of which 123 resolve to one class and 97 span two or more. In
total, 173 assignments (10.7\%) contribute to more than one primary class for one
of these two reasons, while the large majority --- a single CWE mapping to a
single class --- contribute to exactly one.

\section{RQ1: Analysis of Vulnerability Classes in Home IoT Devices}
\label{sec:rq1}

To answer RQ1, we refer to Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-matrix}, which maps every
CWE attributed to a confirmed home IoT device CVE onto the CWE-888 primary
vulnerability classes (Section~\ref{sec:method-cwe888}). The table lists the 22

the \texttt{Malware} column is retained but empty. Results are broken down by
category. The corpus behind it is 1{,}738 confirmed
CVE--category assignments over 1{,}676 distinct CVEs, yielding 1{,}904 mapped
CWE attributions across the 22 categories with confirmed data.
Figures~\ref{fig:cwe888-treemap-all}--\ref{fig:cwe888-treemap-evcharging}
show the same distribution as area-proportional treemaps --- one overall and
one each for the four categories with the most confirmed CVEs (\texttt{cameras},
\texttt{hub}, \texttt{alarms}, \texttt{ev-charging}, the only categories above
$N=75$) --- mirroring Figs.~2--4 of the transportation IoT study. Unlike
Table~III of the transportation IoT study, which lists 23 classes as rows
against 4 device categories as columns, this study's categories are shown as
rows against the CWE-888 classes as columns, so that each category's own
dominant classes read across a single line; the shaded cells in each row
(\colorbox{yellow}{\phantom{0}}) mark that category's 6 most common classes,
the same highlighting convention the transportation study uses to identify
its dominant classes per column.

Two findings stand out at the corpus level. First, a small number of classes
account for most of the corpus: \texttt{Tainted Input} (23\%) and
\texttt{Memory Access} (22\%) together cover almost half of all attributions,
followed by \texttt{Authentication} (14\%) and \texttt{Information Leak}
(10\%); the six most common classes account for 81\% of every attribution we
observe. This is a markedly input-validation- and memory-centric profile,
consistent with a device population built largely on embedded C firmware
exposed through network-facing parsers and web interfaces. Second, the
classes that \emph{recur} are not quite the ones that \emph{dominate}.
\texttt{Information Leak} appears in 21 of the 22 categories and
\texttt{Tainted Input} in 19, while \texttt{Memory Access} --- the second
largest class by volume --- appears in only 14. Memory-safety defects are
therefore concentrated rather than universal: they are the signature of
specific device classes, not a property of home IoT as a whole.

That concentration is visible in the per-category rows. \texttt{Memory
Access} accounts for 61\% of \texttt{hub} attributions, 39\% of
\texttt{smartspeakers}, and 36\% of \texttt{streaming} --- the three
categories whose devices run the most substantial general-purpose software
stacks --- against 17\% for \texttt{cameras}. The camera-style categories
instead lead with \texttt{Tainted Input} (29\% for \texttt{cameras}, 40\% for
\texttt{alarms}), reflecting the web-management and RTSP/ONVIF interfaces
those devices expose. \texttt{Access Control} peaks in \texttt{doorlock}
(21\%), the one category where the access-control decision \emph{is} the
device's function. Reading the top-6 share as a concentration measure, the
larger categories cluster tightly in the 76--91\% range, with \texttt{pet}
(76\%) the most diffuse and \texttt{hub} (91\%) the most concentrated.

The top-6 figures of 100\% in \texttt{fans}, \texttt{fridge},
\texttt{sensors}, \texttt{airpurifier}, \texttt{appliances}, and
\texttt{airconditioner} are an artifact of small $N$ rather than a finding:
each of those categories rests on five or fewer confirmed CVEs, so six
classes trivially exhaust them, and a cell reading ``\texttt{Channel} 67\%''
in \texttt{sensors} represents two attributions. We therefore do not
interpret those rows individually, and Section~\ref{sec:rq1-cat1} onward
analyzes the folding categories of Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-family}, which
aggregate them to a level where the percentages are supportable.

Because one category (\texttt{cameras}) supplies 52\% of all attributions,
Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-matrix} reports the overall profile under both
weightings. The micro row pools every attribution, so it describes the
average \emph{vulnerability}; the macro row averages the 22 per-category
profiles with equal weight, so it describes the average \emph{category}. The
two agree on the leading class and on \texttt{Authentication} at 14\%, and
diverge most on \texttt{Memory Access} (22.0\% micro against 14.7\% macro, a
7.3-point gap) and \texttt{Information Leak} (10.4\% against 16.2\%). The
direction of both gaps restates the concentration finding above: memory
defects are over-represented in the largest categories and information
leakage is spread more evenly across small ones. We report the gap as a
robustness check rather than a result --- at 7.3 points it is modest, and it
narrows to 5.3 points once categories are folded
(Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-family}), which is the expected effect of pooling
small categories into better-populated ones.

The subsections that follow discuss the four largest folding categories of
Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-family}, which between them carry 1{,}558 of the 1{,}904
attributions (82\%). The folding categories are the coarser grouping defined
over the 24 leaf categories in the device ontology
(Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}); we discuss results at this level because six
leaf categories rest on five or fewer confirmed CVEs and cannot support
per-category interpretation, while the folded groups are populated enough to
compare. Per-category numbers remain in Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-matrix}
throughout, and the folding never re-labels a CVE --- it is a view over the
same judgments.

\subsection{Cameras and Monitors}
\label{sec:rq1-cat1}

The \texttt{cameras}, \texttt{doorbell}, and \texttt{babymonitor} categories
fold into a single group of 948 confirmed CVEs and 1{,}052 CWE attributions,
55\% of the corpus and by a wide margin the largest group in the study. Its
profile is led by \texttt{Tainted Input} (28\%), followed by \texttt{Memory
Access} (16\%), \texttt{Authentication} (15\%), and \texttt{Information Leak}
(10\%), with a top-6 share of 83\%.

The dominance of \texttt{Tainted Input} is the clearest device-level signal in
our data. These are network-facing devices whose management surface is
typically an embedded web interface and whose media path speaks RTSP or ONVIF,
so they expose an unusually large amount of externally supplied input to
firmware parsers written in C. That the same profile holds across all three
leaf categories --- 29\% for \texttt{cameras}, 21\% for \texttt{doorbell},
18\% for \texttt{babymonitor} --- supports the ontology's decision to fold
them: consumers treat them as three products with distinct brand sets, but
their vulnerability profiles are close enough that the group behaves as one
population. \texttt{Authentication} is the second consistent thread, at
15--23\% across the three, reflecting the recurring pattern of default
credentials and weak session handling in consumer camera firmware. We note
that \texttt{babymonitor} is the category most affected by the vendor-list
contamination discussed in Section~\ref{sec:threats-internal}, so its 22
attributions should be read as the most provisional of the three.

\subsection{Hubs and Controllers}
\label{sec:rq1-cat2}

The \texttt{hub} category stands alone as a folding category, with 247
confirmed CVEs and 282 attributions (15\% of the corpus). Its profile is the
most distinctive in the study: \texttt{Memory Access} alone accounts for 61\%
of its attributions, more than any other class in any other group, and its
top-6 share of 91\% is the highest of the four groups discussed here.

This is a genuine architectural difference rather than a sampling artifact.
Hubs and controllers run the most substantial software stacks of any device
class in scope --- full Linux systems bridging Zigbee, Z-Wave, Thread, and IP,
often with a local automation runtime --- and that additional native code
surface is where memory-safety defects accumulate. The contrast with the
camera group is the sharpest result in Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-family}:
\texttt{Memory Access} is 61\% here against 16\% there, while
\texttt{Tainted Input} inverts, at 10\% here against 28\% there. Cameras are
compromised through what they parse; hubs are compromised through how they are
built. The security implication also differs in kind, because a hub is by
definition a control surface for other devices, so a memory-safety defect
there is a pivot into every device it manages rather than a compromise of one
endpoint.

\subsection{Energy}
\label{sec:rq1-cat3}

The \texttt{ev-charging} and \texttt{home-power} categories fold into an
energy group of 116 confirmed CVEs and 135 attributions (7\%). The ontology
keeps these two leaves separate --- they share no confirmed CVE and their
vendor sets are disjoint --- but folds them for reporting, and their combined
profile is the most evenly spread of the four groups: \texttt{Tainted Input}
(19\%), \texttt{Authentication} (16\%), \texttt{Information Leak} (13\%), and
\texttt{Memory Access} (12\%), with the lowest top-6 concentration of the
group at 78\%.

That flatness is itself the finding. Where cameras and hubs each have a single
defect class accounting for a quarter to two-thirds of their attributions,
energy devices distribute across the full range, which is consistent with a
category that spans two quite different architectures: vehicle-charging
equipment with OCPP-style backend connectivity on one side, and inverters and
home battery systems with cloud-linked monitoring on the other. The elevated
\texttt{Authentication} share (16\%, against 14\% corpus-wide) is notable
given that these devices mediate physical electrical load, where an
authentication bypass has consequences beyond information disclosure.

\subsection{Alarms and Sensors}
\label{sec:rq1-cat4}

The \texttt{alarms} and \texttt{sensors} categories fold into a group of 93
confirmed CVEs and 89 attributions (5\%), though the group is effectively the
\texttt{alarms} category: \texttt{sensors} contributes only 3 attributions and
is one of the six under-populated leaves noted above.

The group's profile is the most \texttt{Tainted Input}-heavy in the study at
38\%, ahead even of the camera group, and it is the only group in which
\texttt{Channel} appears among the leading classes (16\%, against 2\%
corpus-wide). That \texttt{Channel} share is the substantive result here: it
covers defects in the communication path itself, and it surfaces in alarm
systems because they rely on wireless sensor links --- frequently
proprietary sub-GHz protocols --- whose confidentiality and integrity
properties have historically been weak. An alarm system is also the category
where a channel-level defect maps most directly onto the threat the device
exists to counter, since suppressing or spoofing a sensor link defeats the
system without touching its software at all.

\section{RQ2: Analysis of CVSS Scores and Severity}
\label{sec:rq2}

In this section we analyze the CVSS scores and severity levels of the confirmed
home IoT device CVEs, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cvss-summary}. The CVSS score
ranges from 0 to 10 and is a composite metric that integrates the exploitability
and impact of a vulnerability~\cite{ref-cvss}. Severity is derived from the score
using the standard qualitative bands described in Section~\ref{sec:method-extract}.
Every CVE in the analysis set carries a base score, so no category loses rows to
missing data here.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:cvss-summary}, among the 17 categories with at least
five scored CVEs the highest median CVSS score is found in \texttt{hub}
(median~9.8, mean~8.73, $n=247$), followed by \texttt{alarms} (8.8, 8.06, $n=89$)
and a tie at 8.1 between \texttt{garden} and \texttt{pet}. The lowest medians are
\texttt{airconditioner} (6.8, 7.24, $n=5$) and \texttt{doorlock} (6.95, 7.16,
$n=36$); \texttt{robotvacuum} has the lowest mean of any tested category (6.57)
despite a median of 7.4. We conducted a Kruskal--Wallis test~\cite{kruskal1952} across
these 17 categories and obtained $H=169.820$ ($df=16$), $p=9.1\times10^{-28}$, rejecting
the null hypothesis that there is no difference among the categories' CVSS
scores. Five categories --- \texttt{fans}, \texttt{fridge}, \texttt{sensors},
\texttt{airpurifier}, and \texttt{appliances} --- have fewer than five scored CVEs
($n \le 4$ each) and were excluded from the test; they are reported descriptively
in Table~\ref{tab:cvss-summary} (marked~$\dagger$), but no statistical conclusion
should be drawn from them individually.

The results of Dunn's post-hoc test~\cite{dunn1964} (rank-based, tie-corrected, two-sided,
Bonferroni-adjusted across all $\binom{17}{2}=136$ pairwise comparisons) are
dominated by a single category: 13 pairs are significant after correction, and
\emph{12 of them involve} \texttt{hub}, in every case with \texttt{hub} scoring
higher. The strongest are \texttt{hub} versus \texttt{cameras}
($p_{\text{bonf}}=1.5\times10^{-22}$), \texttt{ev-charging}
($p_{\text{bonf}}=2.8\times10^{-12}$), \texttt{streaming}
($p_{\text{bonf}}=3.7\times10^{-11}$), and \texttt{robotvacuum}
($p_{\text{bonf}}=1.8\times10^{-9}$); \texttt{hub} also scores significantly
higher than \texttt{alarms} ($p_{\text{bonf}}=6.7\times10^{-3}$), the
second-ranked category. The single significant pair not involving \texttt{hub} is
\texttt{alarms} versus \texttt{robotvacuum} ($p_{\text{bonf}}=0.010$). The full
136-pair table is available in the accompanying data release (see the
Data Availability statement).

\texttt{hub} therefore stands out on every measure: the highest median and mean,
and a Critical-severity share of 53.4\% (132 of 247 CVEs), meaning most hub
vulnerabilities in our corpus are rated Critical rather than merely High. This is
consistent with the hub's structural position --- it holds credentials for, and
issues commands to, every device bound to it, so a hub compromise escalates into
control of the devices behind it. \texttt{alarms} shows the same pattern less
sharply (41.6\% Critical), which fits devices whose entire purpose is to resist
an adversary. At the other end, \texttt{lighting} has the highest concentration
in the High band of any category (67.6\%) alongside one of the lowest Critical
shares (5.9\%), and \texttt{ev-charging} and \texttt{doorlock} sit at 11.3\% and
11.1\% Critical respectively.

\emph{This finding carries an important caveat.} The \texttt{hub} category is
heavily concentrated in a single vendor: 111 of its 247 confirmed CVEs (44.9\%)
are attributed to Insteon products, with Samsung SmartThings a distant second at
16.2\%. The category's severity profile therefore substantially reflects one
vendor's disclosure history rather than an established property of home hubs as a
device class, and the Dunn comparisons involving \texttt{hub} should be read with
that concentration in mind. We report the result because it is what the corpus
shows, but a replication on a hub population with a broader vendor base is needed
before treating hub severity dominance as a general finding.

A second caveat concerns score comparability. The distribution pools CVSS v2.0,
v3.x, and v4.0 base scores (Section~\ref{sec:method-extract}), and v2.0 uses a
different formula that skews low, making it a confound rather than mere noise. As
a sensitivity check we re-ran the analysis excluding the 81 CVEs scored only under
v2.0, leaving 1{,}657 scored CVEs. The omnibus result is unchanged in direction
and slightly stronger ($H=181.181$, $p=4.9\times10^{-30}$, same 17 categories),
so the conclusions above do not rest on the pooling decision.

Unlike the transportation IoT study, which manually extracted the most
vulnerable \emph{components} (e.g.\ web interface, RKE system, infotainment
system) from each CVE description and cross-tabulated them against severity (its
Table~IV), this study has not yet performed an analogous component-level
extraction. \stub[A component-level breakdown, mirroring Table~IV of the
transportation study, is left as future work (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}) and
would let us test whether specific attack surfaces --- e.g.\ companion mobile
apps, cloud APIs, or physical Zigbee/BLE radio stacks --- concentrate the
Critical/High-severity vulnerabilities the way RKE and web-interface components
did in that study.]

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Construct Validity}
\label{sec:threats-construct}

Our operationalization of ``home IoT device'' rests on five definitional criteria
(Section~\ref{sec:method-scope}), and those criteria require judgment calls at the
margin: whether a streaming device's platform genuinely acts as a home-control surface,
say, or whether a brand's product line is residential rather than commercial or
industrial. We mitigated this by freezing the category list before large-scale collection
and by pinning a shared, written scope note per category (\texttt{categories.csv})
that every reviewer, human or AI, judges against. Even so, the underlying category
boundaries are themselves a modeling choice, and a different set of criteria could redraw
them.

Two further checks bound this threat. First, encoding the five criteria as OWL axioms
(Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}) lets us test whether our published scope decisions
actually follow from our stated criteria, rather than from case-by-case intuition. An
OWL~2~RL reasoner over the criterion facets reproduces our in-scope and out-of-scope
rulings, so no category is in the study for reasons its own criteria do not support. On
its own that is a weak result, because most of those rulings are in-scope categories that
the axiom admits by construction. The check with real force is the deletion test of
Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}: removing any one of the five criteria from the membership
axiom causes the reasoner to object, because a defined-but-excluded boundary type fails on
that criterion alone. All five criteria are load-bearing under this test, so none is
decorative. Both remain consistency checks rather than validity proofs --- the facet values
are themselves assigned by us, so the reasoner confirms that our decisions are internally
coherent, not that the criteria are the right ones. Second, aligning the categories to established
smart-home vocabularies quantifies how far the scope departs from existing practice. The
alignment procedure and the vocabularies searched are described in
Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}. Of the 24 categories, 7 (29\%) have an
exactly corresponding external class; the remaining 17 (71\%) can be denoted only by a
coarser one.

Two distinct gaps sit inside that figure, and the second is the more consequential. Only
a generic superclass --- \texttt{saref:Device}, \texttt{saref:Appliance},
\texttt{saref:Sensor} or \texttt{saref:Actuator} --- denotes 6 of the 24 categories
(25\%): door locks, EV charging, pet devices, robot vacuums, refrigerators and air
purifiers. These are small categories, carrying 10.0\% of our confirmed CVEs. The larger
problem is granularity rather than absence. A single class,
\texttt{s4bldg:AudioVisualAppliance}, denotes five of our categories --- cameras, baby
monitors, doorbells, smart speakers and streaming devices --- which together carry 61.1\%
of our confirmed CVEs. Device types with distinct brand sets, threat models and CVE
profiles collapse into one class, and the home-control role by which streaming devices and
smart speakers enter our scope at all is not expressible in these vocabularies. This
supports the choice to define categories from consumer product boundaries rather than
adopt an existing vocabulary, but it is equally a limitation on comparability: our
categories cannot be mapped one-to-one onto studies built on those vocabularies. We note
one limit of the manifest check itself: it verifies that the IRIs we cite exist, and can
never detect a class we failed to look for --- an earlier version of this analysis stopped
at SAREF core and consequently overstated the absence of denoting classes.

Mapping individual CWEs onto the CWE-888 Software Fault Pattern clusters
(Section~\ref{sec:method-cwe888}) is a second construct-validity threat. When a CWE is not
itself in CWE-888, we map through its parent CWEs, and a CWE whose parents span two
primary clusters is counted in both, which can inflate cluster totals relative to a
stricter one-CWE-one-cluster scheme. We follow the same mapping convention as prior
work~\cite{ref-mcsoftware} to keep the two studies comparable, but both the choice of view
(CWE-888 over, e.g., CWE-699) and the parent-resolution rule shape the resulting ``big
picture'' of vulnerability classes.

\subsection{Internal Validity}
\label{sec:threats-internal}

The vendor and keyword searches are text-based over CVE descriptions and CPE strings,
so classification errors are possible in either direction. A generic brand name can
collide with an unrelated product (the per-term precision analysis,
Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}, found 20 of 426 judged term--category--method
combinations at or below the
0.10-precision prune threshold, e.g.\ vendor terms that matched only unrelated enterprise
products in every judged row), and a device-relevant CVE can contain none of our search
terms at all. We addressed the first failure mode with the three-AI-plus-human review of
every difference-set row and with the term-precision pass that flags terms for pruning.
We addressed the second only partially, through the CPE-expansion step
(Section~\ref{sec:method-collect}), which finds additional CVEs attributed to
already-confirmed device products independent of description wording. The review is
complete: all 8{,}124 reviewed rows carry a settled label, with none
outstanding. A residual error rate nonetheless remains even on the rows that never
reached a human. We validated the strong-consensus relaxation at 99.7\% human agreement
(293 of 294 rows), so on the order of one in a few hundred high-confidence AI-consensus
rows may still be misclassified, and that residual is concentrated in exactly the
high-false-positive categories (cameras, baby monitor, air conditioner) where the
reviewers face the hardest calls. Reviewer disagreement is itself a threat. Codex is
systematically more conservative, rejecting unfamiliar security-focused brands, while
Gemini/Gemma is systematically more liberal, accepting function-overlap across categories
(e.g.\ IP camera $\to$ baby monitor). For that reason we treat Gemini as the weaker third
vote and exclude its confidence from the human-review flag; its judgment still counts
toward the unanimity check, however, so residual bias from either model can still shape
which rows are, and are not, escalated to a human.

\subsection{External Validity}
\label{sec:threats-external}

Our dataset is bounded by NVD's CVE disclosure and CPE-tagging practices, not by actual
real-world deployment or exploitation. A device with no publicly disclosed CVE is
invisible to this study, however insecure it may be in practice, and smaller or newer
vendors are structurally under-represented relative to established brands with active CVE
reporting histories and extensive CPE tagging. CPE data is also known to lag on recent
(2024--2026) CVEs (Section~\ref{sec:method-review}), so the most recent portion of the
corpus is more likely to be classified from description text alone. The snapshot is a
single fixed point in time (2026-08-05), so any vulnerability disclosed after that date is
out of scope by construction, and the study reports a static ``as-of'' picture rather than
a continuously updated one. Finally, since both search methods are text-based, a
device-relevant CVE whose description and CPE strings contain none of our vendor or
keyword terms never enters the dataset, and the CPE-expansion step reaches only products
already confirmed in scope. The analysis therefore characterizes the CVEs our terms
surfaced for each category, not every CVE that may affect it.

\subsection{Reliability}
\label{sec:threats-reliability}

To support reproducibility, all searches run against one fixed, dated, and documented
NVD snapshot (Section~\ref{sec:method-collect}) rather than the live NVD API, so the
corpus a reader would reconstruct today matches the one analyzed here. Every
difference-set judgment, human or AI, is persisted in a store keyed by (category, CVE),
so the review can be audited, re-run, or extended without silently losing or duplicating
prior work. The one part of the pipeline that is not fully deterministic is the
Gemini/Gemma reviewer, whose free-tier model (\texttt{gemma-4-31b-it}) may be swapped or
updated over time. We mitigate this by keeping one model fixed across an entire review
pass and recording which model produced each Gemini judgment.

Reliability of the \emph{ontology facets} is a separate question from reliability of the
CVE judgments, and we measured it directly rather than assuming it. Three independent
annotators labelled a shared subsample of sampled devices, yielding 471 facet--item
judgments over 35--40 devices per facet, and we report Fleiss' $\kappa$~\cite{fleiss1971}
with bootstrap
confidence intervals (Table~\ref{tab:facet-kappa}). Only 4 of 12 facets clear the
conventional $\kappa \geq 0.60$ bar. We therefore treat the facet vocabulary as
\emph{organizing structure} rather than as evidence: facets group categories and generate
hypotheses, and no finding in this paper rests on a facet as its grouping variable. All
496 facet assertions in the published ontology are recorded as \texttt{Estimated}
provenance, and the build gate prints that mix on every run so it cannot fade from view.

Three results here are worth stating explicitly, because each bounds a claim we might
otherwise have made. First, \texttt{supportLifetime} scores \emph{below chance}
($\kappa = -0.311$, 12.5\% raw agreement, 28.3\% ``unsure''). That is not a difficult
facet but a broken instrument: annotators are either reading the value definitions
differently or answering a question the input cannot support, namely a vendor's support
policy inferred from a product name. More annotation would not repair it. Second,
\texttt{hasWebAdminUI} (0.349) and \texttt{computeTier} (0.384) sit at the bottom of the
table, and they are precisely the two facets an independent attempt to \emph{derive}
facet values from CVE description text also failed on. Text derivation and human
annotation share no mechanism, so their agreement about which facets are unassignable is
considerably stronger evidence than either result alone. This is also why we report no
contrast grouped by web-admin presence: the derivation attempt found the apparent effect
size to be governed by the author's choice of keyword list, with 19 of 22 categories
swinging by more than 25 points between a narrow and a loose word list. Third,
\texttt{capturesAV}~--- the facet at the center of our device-heterogeneity
correction~--- fails reliability ($\kappa = 0.386$) as well as validity, so we report it
only as a distribution and never as a category-level value.

Reliability presupposes validity, and the two can fail independently: three annotators
can agree perfectly on a value that is false of the devices it is stamped onto. Because
our facets are asserted per \emph{category} but joined onto \emph{CVE} rows, a category
holding two device subfamilies has a facet value that is a fiction on part of its rows.
We measured this separately by sampling devices within each category (272 devices across
the 10 categories with enough CVE mass to sample) and computing each cell's modal share
(Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}). Of the 120 measured cells, 86 are defensible as a
single category-level value, 22 are usable only for grouping, and 12 are not usable at
all. Cells failing that
gate are withheld from analysis \emph{in code} rather than by policy note, and a second
withholding route catches cells where two reviewers split along a subfamily line in
categories too small to sample at all. This is why \texttt{capturesAV} appears here as a
withheld distribution rather than as a headline number.

We do not report a facet-level triage of human reviewer disagreement, which an earlier
plan for this work proposed. Attributing each two-reviewer split to the facet that drove
it would require using facets as the grouping variable for a reliability analysis, and
the $\kappa$ panel above establishes that most of them are not reliable enough to carry
that weight. Reporting such a triage would invert the evidentiary order, explaining a
measured disagreement in terms of an unmeasured one.

The repository includes: the vendor/brand and keyword term
lists used for collection (\texttt{vendor\_terms.csv}, \texttt{keyword\_terms.csv}); the
raw per-category search outputs (\texttt{results\_all\_<category>.csv},
\texttt{keyword\_<category>.csv}); the offline search engine and pipeline scripts (CVE

leaf-to-folding-category map used for the rollups in Section~\ref{sec:rq1}). We also

expresses every CVE, product, vendor, category assignment, and CWE-888 class as linked
data, so the counts reported in this paper can be reproduced by SPARQL query rather than
by re-running the pipeline. The one artifact not tracked in the repository is the bulk NVD
snapshot itself (\texttt{nvd\_all.csv}, 373{,}518 CVE records merged from the NVD 2.0
API as of 2026-08-05), which is excluded for size rather than for access reasons; the
snapshot's provenance and exact rebuild procedure are documented in
\texttt{data/nvd-snapshot/SNAPSHOT.md} so that it can be regenerated in full from public NVD
data. The repository is public and carries no access restrictions. Rebuilding the snapshot
requires a free NVD API key, and re-running the automated third reviewer requires a Gemini
API key; both are read from a local, gitignored \texttt{.env} file and are not themselves
part of the dataset, so every artifact needed to reproduce the analysis from the published
derived data is included without credentials.
