\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Defining and scoping home IoT}
\label{sec:related-def}
Our operational definition of a home IoT device follows the residential-deployment
framing of Balta-Ozkan et al.~\cite{balta2013} and the home-based IoT security
evaluation of Alrawi et al.~\cite{alrawi2019}, whose four-component (device, mobile app,
cloud, communication) deployment model and hub-in/router-out scoping our criteria
inherit. Prior taxonomies classify home IoT by \emph{function} rather than keyword
alone: Bugeja et al.~\cite{bugeja2018} build an empirical taxonomy of 1{,}193 commercial
smart-home products organized by functional area (security, climate, appliances,
entertainment); Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2019} analyze devices on real home networks and
define device classes that explicitly separate computers, phones, and routers from home
IoT; and FakhrHosseini et al.~\cite{fakhrhosseini2025} survey expert perspectives on
home-automation taxonomy. On the standards side, NIST's consumer-IoT reviews and
baselines~\cite{nistir8267,nistir8259a,nistir8425} enumerate the same retail categories
(cameras, doorbells, plugs, thermostats, televisions) and define an IoT device as a
system with at least one transducer and one network interface, supporting our
connectivity criterion. Hammi et al.~\cite{hammi2022} frame smart-home vulnerabilities
around physical risks to residents---burglary, occupancy inference, and device
hijacking---matching our consumer-ownership criterion.

\subsection{Consumer versus commercial and industrial scope}
\label{sec:related-scope}
A recurring boundary in our review is that enterprise, industrial, and commercial
devices are out of scope even when they share a brand or keyword with consumer products.
This distinction is well established: Xenofontos et al.~\cite{xenofontos2022} present an
attack taxonomy separating consumer, commercial, and industrial IoT with distinct threat
models; Wurm et al.~\cite{wurm2016} compare consumer home-automation devices against
industrial smart-meter and operational-technology equipment; and Alladi et
al.~\cite{alladi2020} and Ugwuanyi and Irvine~\cite{ugwuanyi2021} reinforce a
consumer-grade scope through case studies and consumer/industrial threat-landscape
analysis~\cite{alahmadi2023}. On the networking axis, Stanford's minimum-security
standard~\cite{stanford_iot} explicitly excludes routers, switches, and access points
from its IoT definition, supporting our decision to treat pure-transport gear as
threat-model context rather than as study subjects.

\subsection{Device-category studies}
\label{sec:related-cat}
Individual categories in our set are recognized as distinct device classes in prior
work. For cameras, doorbells, and baby monitors, Dahmen et al.~\cite{dahmen2017} and
NIST~\cite{nistir8267} treat video doorbells as separate from standalone cameras, while
Valente et al.~\cite{valente2019} and Albrecht and McIntyre~\cite{albrecht2015} analyze
dedicated baby monitors separately from enterprise surveillance, and
Bugeja et al.~\cite{bugeja2018cameras} perform a global assessment of internet-exposed smart
cameras. Locks, sensors, and alarms appear as core subjects in the smart-home platform
analyses of Fernandes et al.~\cite{fernandes2016} and the security/safety taxonomies of
Bugeja et al.~\cite{bugeja2018} and Hammi et al.~\cite{hammi2022}. Climate devices
(thermostats, HVAC) are covered by Kumar et al.~\cite{kumar2019}, and bedside sleep
monitors---distinct from wrist wearables---by Apthorpe et
al.~\cite{apthorpe2017}. Plugs, lighting, and appliances are dissected
experimentally by Notra et al.~\cite{notra2014}, Loi et al.~\cite{loi2017}, Davis et
al.~\cite{davis2020}, and Ali and Awad~\cite{ali2018}. Hubs, voice assistants,
and streaming surfaces are treated as home-control device classes by Alrawi et
al.~\cite{alrawi2019}, Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2019}, Vetrivel et al.~\cite{vetrivel2023},
the voice-control taxonomy~\cite{hewitt2022}, and Fernandes et
al.~\cite{fernandes2016}. Energy, pet, and outdoor categories draw on residential
EV-charging security~\cite{johnson2022,johnson2022sandia} and systematic reviews of smart-home
device types~\cite{builgil2023}. Because companion apps are in scope for our review, we
also draw on the mobile-companion-app vulnerability
literature~\cite{mauro2019,neupane2022,schmidt2023}. Broader threat-landscape context
is provided by the Mirai botnet analysis~\cite{antonakakis2017}, smart-home security
surveys~\cite{yang2022,vardakis2024}, and the OWASP IoT project~\cite{owasp}.

\subsection{NVD-based and AI-assisted vulnerability characterization}
\label{sec:related-method}
Methodologically, our pipeline is closest to recent work that mines NVD for IoT
vulnerabilities and confronts the same false-positive problem. Rezaei et
al.~\cite{rezaei2026} (LLIoT) combine a written IoT definition, keyword filtering, a
triple-expert 300-CVE ground truth, and an LLM labeler over the full 2013--2024 CVE set,
reporting LLM agreement around 95\%---directly motivating our blind multi-model review,
though we differ by using three blind AI reviewers plus human adjudication rather than a
single production LLM. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2025} filter ${\sim}198$k CVEs to IoT via
keyword search plus manual verification and analyze high-risk vulnerabilities, and Singla
et al.~\cite{singla2023} label CPE \texttt{vendor:product} pairs into
compute/IoT/networking bins with keyword-plus-human labeling---closest to our CPE-centric
expansion step. FirmRec~\cite{xiao2024} likewise seeds from NVD using mixed vendor and
keyword terms, but for firmware rather than a home-scoped dual method. We differ from all
of these in restricting scope to home IoT across 24 categories, running two independent
discovery methods (vendor and keyword) reconciled per category, and following the
CWE-888 profiling approach of prior empirical vulnerability studies~\cite{ref-mcsoftware}.

\section{Our Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

This section describes the methodology used in the paper. We began by fixing what
counts as a home IoT device, freezing the resulting category list, and publishing
the scope criteria as an OWL ontology so that membership follows from stated
criteria rather than case-by-case intuition. We then identified the device types
belonging to each category and collected the CVEs reported about them in
NVD~\cite{ref-nvd}. Collection relied on two complementary text-search methods, a
vendor/brand search and a device-keyword search, both run offline through a single
engine against one fixed NVD snapshot. We reconciled their results per category
into three disjoint directions --- the two one-sided differences and their
intersection --- and added a fourth through a CPE-based expansion step that deepens
coverage of already-confirmed devices. Because both term lists are hand-compiled and
therefore incomplete in ways their authors cannot see, three automated passes mine
the snapshot for terms the lists are missing; every candidate they propose is vetted
by hand before it enters a list. For each CVE we extracted its characteristics,
including the corresponding CWEs and CVSS information. Following a previous
study~\cite{ref-mcsoftware}, we grouped the individual CWEs into the primary
clusters of the CWE-888 Software Fault Patterns view~\cite{ref-cwe888} to build a
`big picture' of the security vulnerabilities in home IoT devices. Because keyword-
and brand-based NVD searches are text-based, and therefore noisy, every candidate
vulnerability went through a structured false-positive review before we analyzed it.
We close the quality loop from both ends: a per-term precision pass prunes the terms
that generate the noise, and a capture--recapture estimate bounds what our terms
never reached at all.

The empirical work presented in this paper follows the case study (i.e.,
observational) approach based on publicly available data in NVD that are entered
and validated by experts and are widely used by researchers and practitioners.
Using an experimental approach (e.g., penetration testing) or a survey (e.g.,
collecting expert opinions) are complementary empirical approaches that are out
of the scope of this paper.

\subsection{Scope: what counts as a home IoT device}
\label{sec:method-scope}

We define a \emph{home IoT device} as an internet-connected sensor, appliance,
or embedded system deployed within a residential environment for the purpose of
monitoring, automation, or control, owned and maintained by non-expert consumers
without dedicated IT security oversight~\cite{balta2013,alrawi2019}. For a device type to be in scope it must
satisfy all five definitional criteria: (1)~\emph{connectivity} --- it
communicates over a standard network protocol (TCP/IP, MQTT, CoAP, Zigbee, BLE);
(2)~\emph{device class} --- it is a special-purpose sensor, appliance, or
embedded system, not general-purpose IT (PC, phone, tablet, server, game
console); (3)~\emph{deployment context} --- it is intended for a private
residence, not primarily for enterprise or industrial use; (4)~\emph{function}
--- it qualifies if \emph{either} (a)~its primary purpose is to monitor, automate,
or control the home environment or its systems (climate, security, access,
lighting, appliances, presence), \emph{or} (b)~it serves as a home-control surface
or hub for \emph{other} home IoT devices, discovering, controlling, or displaying
their state (e.g.\ acting as a Matter/Thread controller or border router, running a
voice assistant, or surfacing camera/sensor feeds); general-purpose computing and
pure media playback with no such control role do not qualify; and
(5)~\emph{security context} --- it is owned and
maintained by consumers with no professional security administration. An
operational study-inclusion criterion further requires that the device have a
CPE-identifiable footprint in NVD and be subject to CVE disclosure. Virtually
every networked product satisfies connectivity (criterion~1), so the criteria
that actually decide membership are the device's own function (criterion~4) and
class (criterion~2): a device that merely interoperates with home IoT is not
itself home IoT. This is why entertainment hardware is in scope \emph{only} when
it doubles as a home-control surface under criterion~4(b). Streaming TVs, sticks,
and boxes qualify on that basis, because their platforms act as Matter/Thread
controllers, run assistants, and surface camera/doorbell feeds, as do smart
speakers and displays~\cite{kumar2019,fernandes2016}; game consoles and VR/AR
headsets do not, remaining general-purpose compute that fails criteria~2 and~4.
The networking axis follows the same logic: what matters is whether a device
controls other home IoT devices, not whether it carries their traffic. IoT hubs,
bridges, and Matter/Thread/Zigbee controllers are therefore in scope, while
pure-transport gear (plain routers, modems, ONT, unmanaged switches) is excluded
and appears only as untrusted threat-model context~\cite{alrawi2019,stanford_iot}.

The study analyzes 24 home IoT categories: cameras, doorbells, baby monitors, door
locks, alarms, sensors, thermostats, air conditioners, fans, air purifiers, smart
plugs, lighting, refrigerators, robot vacuums, other appliances, hubs, smart speakers,
sleep trackers, shades, EV charging, home power, garden, pet, and streaming devices.
We treat two device types as separate categories when a consumer would call them
different products \emph{and} their brand sets differ meaningfully, and merge them only
when they are the same product under a different label. Under that rule, smart displays
and soundbars fold into the smart-speakers category, and blinds, curtains, and shutters
fold into a single shades category, while cameras, doorbells, and baby monitors stay
distinct despite their hardware overlap, since their brand sets differ. A few device
types satisfy connectivity but fail the class or function criteria; we define them but
keep them out of the analysis set. These are game consoles and VR/AR headsets
(general-purpose compute, out by criteria~2 and~4) and pure-transport networking such as
routers and modems (out by criterion~4). We also still list the sleep-tracker category,
but it is dominated by wrist wearables (out by criterion~3) with essentially no bedside
monitors, and is pending a rebuild that may drop it altogether. We froze category
membership before large-scale collection, so that a later change to one category forces
a re-run of only that category's chain rather than the whole pipeline. The per-category
\emph{term} lists, by contrast, stay open to refinement (Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}).

\subsection{Formalizing the scope as an ontology}
\label{sec:method-ontology}

The criteria above are also published in machine-readable form as an OWL~2
ontology, which serves three purposes in this study. First, it makes the scope
decision auditable: each of the 24 in-scope categories and each defined-but-excluded
type carries an explicit value for all five criteria, and criteria~1--5 are stated as an
equivalence axiom, so an OWL reasoner --- rather than the prose --- decides membership.
Reproducing our published in-scope and out-of-scope rulings is a weak test on its own,
since most of those rulings are in-scope categories confirming that they are in scope,
which the axiom satisfies by construction. The stronger check is that every criterion is
independently load-bearing. We verify this by deleting each of the five conjuncts of the
membership axiom in turn and requiring the reasoner to object, which it can only do if
some defined type fails on precisely that criterion. Five defined-but-excluded boundary
types serve that role, one per criterion: a non-networked detector (criterion~1), a
tablet control panel (2), a commercial HVAC controller (3), pure-transport networking
gear (4), and a professionally monitored alarm service (5). All five criteria are
load-bearing under this test; we report it as a construct-validity check in
Section~\ref{sec:threats-construct}. Second, it records
the admission route, not merely the verdict. Because criterion~4 admits a device either
by direct home control (4(a)) or by acting as a control surface for other devices (4(b)),
and because 4(b) is the contested clause that lets entertainment hardware in, the
distinction is worth quantifying: 1{,}624 of the 1{,}738 confirmed CVE--category
assignments enter through 4(a) and only 114 through 4(b), so the boundary that attracts
the most methodological objection governs 6.6\% of the corpus. Third, it defines a
coarser tier of 13 \emph{folding categories} over the 24 leaves, used for reporting
wherever a leaf category is too sparse to interpret (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}). The
ontology generates \texttt{categories.csv} --- the same file that supplies every
reviewer's scope note --- so the formal model and the artifact the pipeline actually
consumes cannot drift apart. It holds classes and scope notes only, never judgments: no
CVE is classified by the ontology, and no reviewer sees information the prose scope note
did not already contain. Deriving judgments from the ontology instead would make the
term-precision figures of Section~\ref{sec:method-precision} self-confirming, since the
labels being measured would then come from the same model that defined the scope.

Once the review has settled, we export the analyzed corpus itself as an instance graph
against this vocabulary (80{,}072 triples: 1{,}676 vulnerabilities, 1{,}738
category assignments, 4{,}707 device products, 292 vendors and 21 CWE-888 classes). The
export is verified rather than trusted: a gate re-derives every one of those counts, the
per-category totals, the leaf-to-family rollup, and the 1{,}904 CWE-888 attributions from
the judgment store and the CWE mapping used in Sections~\ref{sec:rq1}--\ref{sec:rq2}, so
the published graph cannot drift from the files the analysis runs on. Two of the 24
categories and two of the 13 folding categories carry no confirmed CVE and appear in the
graph as empty classes.

Beyond the five membership criteria, the ontology carries a vocabulary of
\emph{descriptive facets} (for example placement, cloud dependence, whether the device
actuates something physical, and the consequence if it does) used to group categories for
analysis. None of these facets appears in the membership axiom, so none can move a scope
ruling. Their evidentiary status is recorded explicitly and is deliberately modest: all
496 facet assertions in the published ontology are marked \texttt{Estimated}, meaning
hand-assigned from domain knowledge with no external source, and the build gate prints
that mix on every run. We subjected the facet vocabulary to three separate checks, each
of which can disqualify a facet independently, and report the results in
Section~\ref{sec:threats-reliability}. (i)~\emph{Validity.} Facets are asserted per
category but joined onto CVE rows, so we sampled devices within each category (272
devices across the 10 categories with enough CVE mass to sample, 120 category--facet
cells) and measured what share of a cell's devices actually take its asserted value,
banding the result on the same 0.80 and 0.60 cutoffs used for agreement in~(ii). Cells
that fail are withheld from the analysis in code rather than by a note to the reader, and
a second withholding route covers cells where two independent reviewers split along a
device-subfamily line in categories too small to sample. The remaining 14 categories stay
unmeasured rather than being assumed sound, and roughly a fifth of confirmed assignments
carry no device CPE at all and so cannot be product-sampled at any budget.
(ii)~\emph{Reliability.} Three annotators independently labelled a shared subsample of
35--40 devices per facet, 471 facet--item judgments in total, and we report Fleiss'
$\kappa$~\cite{fleiss1971}; only 4 of 12 facets clear the conventional $\kappa \geq 0.60$
bar~\cite{landis1977}. (iii)~\emph{Sourcing.} A per-category pass attempted
to attach an external source to each facet value, settling 185 cells, of which 94 carry a
citation and 91 record a reasoned judgment with no source that supports the whole
category; none reached documented-by-the-vendor status. Because a citation is only
evidence about \emph{this} corpus if it covers it, each cited source is scored by the
share of the category's confirmed CVEs that its vendors account for, and a cell whose
citation falls below that floor is demoted to a judgment with the source retained. Taken
together, these checks are why we treat the facets as organizing structure: they group
categories and generate hypotheses, and no finding in this paper uses one as its grounding
variable.

Two further artifacts sit alongside the class definitions, both verified against pinned
manifests for the same reason. The first is an alignment to established smart-home
vocabularies --- SAREF core~\cite{saref} with its S4BLDG~\cite{saref4bldg} and its S4ENER,
S4WEAR, S4AGRI, S4AUTO, S4CITY and S4EHAW extensions~\cite{saref4ext}, plus W3C
SSN/SOSA~\cite{ssn2017} --- expressed only as subclass and broader-match
relations, never as class equivalence, since our classes are strictly narrower than the
external ones they map to (our camera class is residential consumer IP cameras, whereas
\texttt{saref:Sensor} is any sensor). Every external IRI is checked against a pinned
435-IRI manifest drawn from those vocabularies, each recorded at a fixed version and
content hash, so a reader can reconstruct the exact documents we searched rather than
whichever version of them is current. Two of the eight yielded nothing in scope, which we
record as a result rather than dropping them from the searched set;
Section~\ref{sec:threats-construct} reports what the alignment shows.
The second records, per category, the literature that motivates its inclusion, separating
studies that evaluate the device type by name from those whose method covers it without
naming it. Collapsing that distinction would overstate how well grounded the category set
is: of the 24 categories, 13 (54\%) have a study evaluating them directly, 10 are
supported only methodologically, and \texttt{streaming} has neither. Weighted by confirmed
CVEs, 14.6\% of the analysis set sits on categories that no study in our manifest examines
directly.

\subsection{CVE collection from NVD}
\label{sec:method-collect}

CVEs are publicly known security vulnerabilities reported in NVD~\cite{ref-nvd}
and attributed to specific software applications or libraries. We ran all searches
offline against a single fixed snapshot of NVD, built by merging the per-year
NVD JSON feeds (2000--2026, i.e.\ every CVE in NVD as of the download date) pulled from
the NVD 2.0 API on 2026-08-05, for a total of 373{,}518 unique CVE records. Freezing the
corpus this way serves two purposes: it makes the dataset reproducible and citeable
(``as of'' that date), and it removes data-lag differences between the two searches. We
collected CVEs related to home IoT devices using two complementary text-search methods,
each more reliable in a different respect, augmented them with a CPE-based expansion step,
and mined the snapshot itself for terms the two hand-compiled lists were missing:

\paragraph{Vendor/brand search} For each category we first compiled a list of
the manufacturers and brands that produce devices of that type, then searched the
snapshot for those vendor and brand names. We assembled the brand lists per
category by surveying the major residential smart-home ecosystems---Google
Nest/Home, Apple Home (HomeKit), Amazon Alexa, and comparable hub platforms---and
enumerating the device types and device brands certified to interoperate within
each ecosystem, supplemented by targeted per-category web searches for additional
brands not surfaced through the ecosystems. Where a bare brand name overlaps unrelated
products, we qualified it with a product word (e.g.\ ``carrier infinity'' rather
than ``carrier'') to suppress the obvious noise. Every brand term was matched against
both the CVE \emph{description text and the CPE (Common Platform Enumeration)
strings}, so that brand-in-CPE associations are captured. Matching is whole-word
(tokens must sit on alphanumeric boundaries, with a trailing plural allowed), which
blocks substring false hits --- \texttt{nvr} matching \texttt{nvram}, \texttt{evse}
matching \texttt{prevsell} --- while leaving CPE matching unaffected, since the
punctuation CPE uses to separate its fields counts as a boundary. One matching
concession is specific to CPE: a space in a multi-word term also matches an
underscore, so that \texttt{hunter douglas} reaches
\texttt{hunter\_douglas:\ldots}. Without it a multi-word term could only ever match
the description half of the haystack, which had silently suppressed the CPE
contribution of every multi-word brand. Each output row
also records \emph{which} brand term(s) matched it, which feeds the per-term precision
analysis in Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}. The list also absorbs the hand-vetted
output of the automated discovery passes described below. The final vendor list holds
1{,}564 category--term pairs over 1{,}540 distinct brand strings. Since brand names frequently overlap
with unrelated products, this method returns more candidate matches but is also more
prone to false positives.

\paragraph{Device-keyword search} In parallel, we searched for generic device-type
phrases (e.g.\ ``ip camera'', ``video doorbell'', ``smart lock''), restricted to
device-type language only, with no brands, protocols, or firmware names, since brand
discovery is the vendor search's job. This search runs through the \emph{same offline
engine and against the same fixed snapshot} as the vendor search, matching \emph{both
description text and CPE strings} under the same whole-word rule and emitting the
identical output schema (including matched-term attribution). The keyword list is
correspondingly much smaller than the brand list at 208 device phrases, since it names
device types rather than enumerating manufacturers. The two methods thus
differ only in their search \emph{terms}, device phrases versus brand names, which is
what makes their outputs directly comparable. Each CVE carries its CVSS score and
version, CWE identifiers, CPE strings, and description.

\paragraph{Automated term discovery} Both term lists are hand-compiled, and a
hand-compiled list is incomplete in ways its author cannot introspect: the brands one
does not know about are exactly the ones that never get searched. We therefore mine the
snapshot itself for terms the lists are missing, on each of the three text surfaces
separately. The first mines the CPE \emph{vendor} field for manufacturers that co-occur
with our confirmed devices, weighting evidence from vendors appearing on confirmed
true-match CVEs above vendors appearing merely on keyword-matched but unjudged ones. The
second mines confirmed true-match CVE \emph{descriptions} for device-type n-grams, scored
both for how strongly they discriminate confirmed from rejected rows and for how many
CVEs they would newly bring in, measured through the same matcher the pipeline uses. The
third mines the CPE \emph{product} field for single device nouns, which is where a term
too generic to use as a description keyword becomes precise: \texttt{camera} matches half
of NVD in free text, but a product literally named \texttt{\ldots\_camera\_firmware} is a
camera. That surface is the only one on which several of our vendors were reachable at
all. Each pass discards candidates already covered by an existing term for that category,
scores every survivor for the CVEs it would add that neither text search already found,
and flags the known hazards --- diversified mega-vendors whose footprint dwarfs their
in-scope products, dictionary words, and terms with an established false-positive record.
Flags triage the list; they never filter it. Critically, none of the three passes writes to
a term list: each emits a candidate file, and accepting a term is a human judgment made
against the flags and the yield estimate, which is also why term discovery is not chained
into the automated pipeline. The one vetting pass to date accepted 41 mined terms (17
brand terms and 24 device phrases); the product-noun scan currently runs 47 tokens across
19 categories, and terms accepted from it enter as ordinary brand terms, since the
underscore concession above lets them reach CPE strings directly.

\paragraph{Reconciliation} For each category we combined the two result sets
using set operations, into three disjoint parts that together partition the union of the
two searches. The \emph{difference} we compute \emph{bidirectionally}: \emph{vendor-only}
(vendor $-$ keyword) collects vendor CVEs the keyword search missed, and
\emph{keyword-only} (keyword $-$ vendor) collects keyword CVEs the brand list missed,
which is what surfaces gaps in the vendor/brand list. The \emph{intersection} (vendor
$\cap$ keyword) holds the CVEs both methods found. All three are reviewed, which was not
our original design: we initially treated the intersection as high-precision by
construction, on the reasoning that two independent methods agreeing is itself evidence,
and reviewed only the two differences. An audit of the intersection showed that reasoning
does not hold, and the settled corpus quantifies by how much --- across all categories,
70.2\% of intersection rows are true matches, so close to a third would have entered the
dataset unexamined. The failure is concentrated exactly where a category's generic device
vocabulary collides with a professional product line: 67.0\% in \texttt{cameras}, where
device phrases such as ``ip camera'' match enterprise surveillance brands that also carry
the vendor terms, and 15.8\% in \texttt{smartspeakers}. Method agreement, in other words,
measures that two term lists overlap, not that the device is in scope. Reviewing these
sets serves a dual purpose: it cleans the dataset, and, by mining the confirmed true
matches, it feeds new terms back into whichever list was missing them (keyword phrases
from vendor-only true matches, brands from keyword-only true matches).

\paragraph{CPE expansion} As a further discovery step, working from NVD's own attribution
rather than from text, we expand the set of CVEs attributed to devices already confirmed as
in scope. Seeding from the true-match
(\texttt{Yes}) rows of the review, we take each confirmed device's
\texttt{vendor:product} CPE, restricted to genuine device CPEs (\texttt{vendor:product}
pairs whose \texttt{part} is hardware or operating system, dropping vendor-only and
shared application/library CPEs). We then scan the snapshot for every other CVE that NVD
attributes to that same product and subtract everything the two text searches already
found. The remaining CVEs form a fourth candidate set that flows back through the same
false-positive review; like the mined terms above, they are never accepted automatically.
Three constraints keep the step honest. It may seed only from a settled true match, never
from an unreviewed CVE, so it can never introduce a brand no reviewer has signed off on.
It seeds from \texttt{vendor:product} pairs rather than from a vendor, so a confirmed
smart plug expands to that plug's product line and not to everything its manufacturer
makes. And the \texttt{part} restriction pins expansion to the physical device rather
than its dependencies, dropping the application and library CPEs that ride along on a
device CVE; general-purpose computing platforms are denied outright even though they are
operating systems, because a vendor that attributes one CVE across its entire OS family
would otherwise drag the whole desktop and mobile corpus in behind a single confirmed
device.

Its yield is strongly category-dependent, and the aggregate figure is misleading unless
the scope exclusion below is applied first. The step returned 2{,}068 candidates, of which
reviewers confirmed 1{,}960 --- but 1{,}897 of those confirmations are Apple TV
expansions later removed by the tvOS exclusion, so on the 171 candidates that reach the
analysis set precision is 36.8\% (63 of 171). Within that remainder the split is stark:
every one of the 38 \texttt{alarms} candidates was confirmed, tracing to a single prolific
product with tersely worded CVEs that named no brand our terms would catch, while
\texttt{cameras} confirmed 15 of 67. The method faithfully propagates whatever its seeds
imply, which makes it a high-value densifier for products NVD describes by CPE rather than
by name, and a source of noise wherever a confirmed seed sits in a crowded product
namespace.

Because all searches run against the single fixed snapshot, the collection is a
point-in-time picture as of 2026-08-05 rather than a date range. After reconciliation
and the CPE-expansion step, the two searches together produced 8{,}124 candidate
(category, CVE) pairs across the 24 categories, all of which entered the false-positive
review of Section~\ref{sec:method-review}. Of these, 3{,}753 were confirmed as true
matches to a home IoT device and 4{,}371 were rejected as false positives, with no rows
left outstanding. By provenance, the candidate pairs decompose into 4{,}241 vendor-only,
1{,}049 keyword-only, 766 intersection, and 2{,}068 CPE-expansion rows, and their
precisions differ by a factor of four: 22.9\% of vendor-only and 18.5\% of keyword-only
rows reached the analysis set, against 70.2\% of intersection rows. The two one-sided differences
are therefore where the false-positive load lives, which is what makes reviewing them the
expensive part of the pipeline and pruning their terms
(Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}) the cheapest available saving. A single CVE
that legitimately affects devices in more
than one category is counted once per category, so these are category-CVE pairs rather
than distinct CVE identifiers. The transportation IoT study conservatively excluded
ambiguous Qualcomm chipsets; the analogous deliberate exclusion here was Apple's tvOS.
Although a streaming platform such as Apple TV nominally qualifies as a home-control
surface, tvOS shares a codebase with Apple's general-purpose operating systems (iOS,
macOS, watchOS), so NVD attributes a single such CVE across the entire OS family at
once; tvOS-tagged CVEs would therefore dominate the \texttt{streaming} category by
sheer volume while reflecting shared-platform rather than device-specific weaknesses.
We accordingly mark tvOS CVEs out of scope for \texttt{streaming}
(Section~\ref{sec:method-scope}). This exclusion is substantial and applied after review
rather than before it: it removes 2{,}015 of the 3{,}753 confirmed true matches, every one
of them in \texttt{streaming}. What remains --- 1{,}738 confirmed CVE--category
assignments over 1{,}676 distinct CVEs --- is the analysis set used throughout
Sections~\ref{sec:rq1}--\ref{sec:rq2}. We record the excluded rows as scope exclusions
rather than deleting them or reversing their judgments, so the review outcome and the
scope decision stay separable in the published data.

The review described above establishes the \emph{precision} of this collection but says
nothing about its \emph{recall}, which matters because both methods are text-based and a
device-relevant CVE naming none of our terms never enters the corpus at all. We estimate
recall by treating the vendor and keyword searches as two independent capture occasions of
the same underlying CVE population and applying the Chapman-corrected Lincoln--Petersen
estimator~\cite{chapman1951}, which requires no additional labelling. Pooled across categories this puts
combined search-stage recall at 0.603 (95\% CI 0.556--0.648), over 4{,}849 observed CVEs
against an estimated population of 8{,}046. That figure should be read as an \emph{upper}
bound: the two searches are positively dependent rather than independent, since a CVE
naming a brand often also names the device type, and such dependence biases the estimated
population size downward and hence recall upward.

Because that dependence is the estimate's main weakness, we also fit a three-source
variant that does not have to assume it away. It adds a third capture occasion --- every
CVE NVD attributes to a confirmed-in-scope device CPE --- and selects among Poisson
log-linear models~\cite{fienberg1972} with and without pairwise interaction terms by
AIC~\cite{akaike1974}, letting the data
estimate the vendor--keyword dependence instead of stipulating independence. Of the 22
categories that support any estimate at all, 21 support a three-source fit, and their intervals are wide
enough that we read the per-category figures as indicative rather than precise; the third
set is also not a clean capture occasion, since it can only reach products that already
appeared in one of the two searches. Three structural cases carry no estimate at all, and
we report them rather than filling them in: five categories are \emph{degenerate}, with
one search's results a subset of the other's, so recapture carries no information and the
estimator returns an artifactual 1.0 (these are excluded from the pooled figure); one
category has no in-scope seed and so forms no third set; and two categories have an empty
term list on one side, so no recapture is possible at all. We therefore treat recall as an
order-of-magnitude check on coverage rather than a measurement, and return to its
limitations in Section~\ref{sec:threats-external}.

\paragraph{Comparability} An earlier iteration of this pipeline ran the keyword
search against the live NVD API (matching the description only), while the vendor
search ran offline over a year-feed snapshot (matching description plus CPE). That
asymmetry made apparent ``gaps'' between the methods partly an artifact of data lag and
of the description-versus-CPE match surface, rather than a genuine difference in what
each method found. We eliminated the confound by running \emph{both} term lists through
the same offline engine against the same fixed NVD snapshot, matching description plus
CPE under the same whole-word rule, so that the only difference left between the two
searches is the search terms themselves.

\paragraph{Snapshot vintage} Pinning the snapshot makes the two searches comparable; it
does not mean the corpus can never move. The invariant we hold is that both term lists are
always run against the \emph{same} vintage, which a re-download preserves at a new date so
long as both searches are re-run. We did this once during the study, replacing a 2026-06-25
snapshot of 360{,}981 records with the 2026-08-05 snapshot of 373{,}518 used throughout
this paper, rebuilding both searches and every downstream set against it. Two properties of
the design made that affordable. Because settled judgments persist in a store keyed by
(category, CVE), the full snapshot refresh plus re-search cost 111 genuinely new review
rows out of 5{,}122, with the remainder restored automatically. And because the previous
vintage was retained rather than overwritten, we could measure what NVD had changed on
records we had already analyzed before cutting over --- a comparison that has to be made
prospectively, since NVD serves only current state (Section~\ref{sec:method-extract}).

\subsection{CWE and CVSS data extraction}
\label{sec:method-extract}

Each CVE is typically attributed to one or more CWEs. Some CVEs, however, either
lacked enough information to be associated with a CWE in NVD or fell outside NVD's
CWE mapping; NVD lists these as \texttt{NVD-CWE-noinfo} and \texttt{NVD-CWE-Other},
respectively. Of the 1{,}676 confirmed home IoT CVEs in the analysis set, 118 (7.0\%)
carried no usable CWE mapping (an absent, \texttt{NVD-CWE-noinfo}, or \texttt{NVD-CWE-Other}
value; our merged year-feed snapshot does not preserve the distinction among these
three cases, collapsing all of them to an empty CWE field, so we report them together
rather than splitting the \texttt{noinfo} and \texttt{Other} counts). We kept these in the CVE-level analysis
(Sections~\ref{sec:rq1}--\ref{sec:rq2}), but they do not contribute to the CWE analysis.
The remaining 1{,}558 CVEs carried CWE attributions spanning 155 unique CWE identifiers
before mapping onto the CWE-888 view.

A second, independent gap affects CPE rather than CWE data: 193 of the 1{,}676 confirmed
CVEs (11.5\%) carry no CPE string at all. This is the population that our reviewers had to
classify from description text alone (Section~\ref{sec:method-review}), and it is also
what bounds the CPE-expansion step, which can only seed from CVEs that carry a device CPE.
We report both gaps as standing limitations of the corpus rather than as artifacts of NVD
enrichment lag that a later snapshot would clear. Comparing our snapshot against the
preceding vintage six weeks earlier, NVD had backfilled a CWE on none of the 118 CWE-less
rows and a CPE on 3 of the 196 then CPE-less ones, had changed no base score, had re-scored
none of the 81 v2.0-only records under v3.x, and had newly rejected nothing. All three of
the real changes are 2026 CVEs NVD was analyzing for the first time, so what the comparison
measures is first analysis, not reanalysis: on a fixed set of already-analyzed records, NVD
churn is close to nil, and neither gap is closing at a rate that would change these
figures.

For each CVE we also extracted the CVSS information. CVSS scores range from 0 to
10 and are computed from the vulnerability's exploitability and impact
metrics~\cite{ref-cvss}. Severity follows from the score: a score of 0 is the None
severity level, and the Low, Medium, High, and Critical levels correspond to score
ranges of 0.1--3.9, 4.0--6.9, 7.0--8.9, and 9.0--10, respectively. The corpus mixes CVSS
v2, v3.x, and v4.0 scores. Where a CVE carries metrics under more than one version we
select one, in the fixed preference order v3.1, v3.0, v4.0, v2.0. Placing v4.0 below both
v3 versions is deliberate: mixing base-score versions within a single distribution is a
confound, so we keep the bulk of the corpus on v3.x and fall back to v4.0 only where no v3
metric exists, which is what recovers the CVEs NVD has scored under v4.0 alone. v2.0 stays
last, as the oldest and least comparable standard. Under this rule the 1{,}676 confirmed
CVEs resolve to 1{,}211 v3.1 scores, 330 v3.0, 54 v4.0-only, and 81 v2.0-only. Both base
score and vector string are present for every CVE in the analysis set (1{,}676 of 1{,}676),
so no CVE is dropped from the severity analysis for want of a score. That completeness is
worth attributing correctly, because it came from a correction on our side rather than from
NVD: 57 CVEs in the analysis set had previously extracted as unscored, of which 54 were
scored only under v4.0 and were being missed by a metric reader that never inspected the
v4.0 block, the other 3 being CVEs NVD analyzed for the first time in the interval. Vector
strings were likewise not extracted at all until that pass. A silent parser gap of this
kind is indistinguishable from missing upstream data unless the two vintages are compared
directly, which is the argument for retaining the superseded snapshot. We did not perform
a component-level extraction of the vulnerable component from each CVE description in this study; a
component breakdown mirroring the transportation study's Table~IV is left to future
work (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).

\subsection{Mapping individual CWEs to the CWE-888 view}
\label{sec:method-cwe888}

CWE is a community-developed taxonomy of common weaknesses maintained by
MITRE~\cite{ref-cwe}. The CWE hierarchy is large and complex, and several views
have been developed to organize it. We chose the CWE-888 Software Fault Patterns
view~\cite{ref-cwe888} to categorize the identified CWEs for two reasons:
(i)~it was used in~\cite{ref-mcsoftware}, which keeps our results consistent with,
and directly comparable to, that study; and (ii)~it centers on fault categorization,
is more technical, and captures a wider range of weaknesses than alternatives such
as the CWE-699 Software Development view~\cite{ref-cwe699}. We pin the CWE catalog to
version 4.12 (released 29 June 2023) so that the mapping is reproducible, and focus only
on the view's 23 primary clusters, which we refer to as primary vulnerability classes.
Of those 23, 21 receive at least one attribution from our corpus and 2 receive none;
the empty clusters are retained in the mapping but carry no data in our results.

When an identified CWE is itself part of the CWE-888 view, we mapped it directly to
its corresponding primary class. When it is not, we examined its parent CWEs and mapped
those instead, so a CWE whose parents fall under two different primary classes counts
toward both. This over-counting is bounded in practice.
Of the 1{,}613 (category, CVE) assignments carrying at least one CWE --- 1{,}571 of which
carry a CWE that resolves to at least one primary class, the remaining 42 carrying only
CWEs that resolve to none --- 76 (4.7\%)
have a single CWE whose parents fall under two different primary classes and so count
toward both. A further 220 (13.6\%) list two or more distinct CWEs; of those, 123 resolve
to a single primary class while 97 span two or more. Taken together, 173 assignments
(10.7\%) contribute to more than one primary class for one of these two reasons, while the
large majority (a single CWE mapping to a single class) contribute to exactly one. This
approach builds a more complete picture of the vulnerability types present while keeping
over-counting in check.

\subsection{False-positive classification}
\label{sec:method-review}

Because the vendor and keyword searches are purely text-based, generic brand names
and terms inevitably produce false positives, such as a brand string that also names an
unrelated commercial product, or marketing copy that merely mentions a device category.
We therefore classified each candidate CVE in the difference set independently, as either
a true match for its category or a false positive.

To make this trustworthy, every row was judged \emph{independently and blind} by
three AI reviewers, and the rows
those three left unsettled were blind-adjudicated by two human researchers (Aarav Verma
and Suhani Assija).
% DOUBLE-BLIND: if the venue requires anonymized submission, replace the two names above
% with "two of the authors" and drop the acknowledgement of individual reviewers.
The three AI reviewers were Anthropic's Claude, accessed through
Claude Code (judgments collected 2026-06-01 to 2026-07-02); OpenAI's ChatGPT Codex
(2026-06-15 to 2026-06-30); and Google's \texttt{gemma-4-31b-it}, accessed through the
Gemini API (2026-06-16 to 2026-07-01)\stub[optional: add the exact Claude and Codex model
version strings if they can be recovered]. Each reviewer judged every row in a single pass, with no
self-consistency sampling or re-prompting, and we held one model fixed for the whole of a
given reviewer's column, so that a column reflects one judge rather than a mixture. We
record the collection dates rather than internal model build identifiers for the two
interactively accessed reviewers, since those identifiers were not captured at run time;
this is a reproducibility limitation we note rather than resolve. We enforce
blindness structurally. Each reviewer works on its own copy of the data, containing only
the raw fields plus its own empty judgment columns, so no reviewer can see another's
answer. All reviewers apply a single shared rubric together with a shared per-category
scope note (a short in/out scope statement pinned to each category), and judge \emph{only}
from each row's \texttt{description} and \texttt{cpe\_strings}. Pinning one scope note per
category is what ensures that agreement between reviewers reflects a common scope, rather
than three different readings of a one-word category label.

Each reviewer assigns three fields per row: a \emph{Judgment} of \texttt{Yes}
(genuinely affects a home IoT device of this category), \texttt{No} (false
positive), or \texttt{Maybe} (genuinely ambiguous); a \emph{Confidence} of
\texttt{High} or \texttt{Low}; and a short, self-contained \emph{Reasoning},
required for \texttt{Low}-confidence and \texttt{Maybe} rows. A \texttt{Maybe} always
pairs with \texttt{Low} confidence. We reserve Low confidence for the harder calls:
when the device is plausibly residential but primarily commercial or industrial, when
the description names the category but the CPE points at enterprise hardware, or when the
CVE lies in a software or protocol layer shared between home and non-home contexts. A
missing CPE string does not by itself force a \texttt{Maybe}. CPE data is frequently
absent on recent (2024--2026) CVEs because of NVD enrichment lag, so when a description
unambiguously names a home device with a residential attack vector, we classify it on
that content. The rubric also fixes two recurring boundary calls that reviewers would
otherwise resolve inconsistently. A device's own companion app and vendor cloud API count
as part of the device: a vulnerability that compromises a specific home IoT product
through the app used to provision or control it is in scope for that product's category
even though the fix lands in app code, because the app--device--cloud coupling is a
defining property of a consumer IoT deployment rather than a reason to exclude
it~\cite{alrawi2019}. The rule does not run the other way: an unrelated device that merely
runs an app controlling home IoT still fails the class and function criteria, which is the
guiding principle of Section~\ref{sec:method-scope} restated at row level. And where one
app-layer vulnerability spans several of a vendor's product lines at once --- bulbs, a plug
and a camera behind a single app --- reviewers accept it for every named product line
rather than rejecting it for ambiguity, which is what makes the per-category counts
additive over such a CVE rather than dependent on which category happened to be reviewed
first.

Of the three AI reviewers, two (Claude Code and ChatGPT Codex) are run manually and
the third (Gemini/Gemma) is scripted against the vendor's API. All three judge the
combined review set for a category, which unions the vendor-only, keyword-only,
intersection, and CPE-expansion directions (a \texttt{Difference Type} field on each row
records its origin), and their judgments are then merged. We flag a row for human adjudication when
both strong reviewers (Claude and Codex) are Low confidence, or when the three AI
judgments are not unanimous. We deliberately relax this in one case: a lone Gemini
dissent does \emph{not} flag a row when Claude and Codex both agree on a definite
\texttt{Yes} or \texttt{No} at High confidence. Such rows settle as
\texttt{strong-consensus} and stay in a high-confidence audit pool (carrying a recorded
Gemini-dissent flag) as an ongoing spot check, rather than adding to the human queue. We
validated this relaxation against a fully worked human queue, on which the human
reviewers sided with the Claude--Codex pair on 293 of 294 such rows (99.7\%); that
agreement drops to 95.5\% when the pair agree at any confidence, which is why we require
High confidence on both. We treat Gemini as a weaker third vote: its judgment counts
toward unanimity, but its self-reported confidence is recorded and left out of the flag.
Humans then adjudicate only the flagged rows, and each CVE's judgments collapse into a
single final label (\texttt{ai-consensus} for unflagged rows, \texttt{human} for
adjudicated rows). The human stage mirrors the AI stage in requiring agreement rather than
a single verdict: a flagged row settles only when both human reviewers have answered, agree,
and the shared verdict is not \texttt{Maybe}. A row with one verdict, two conflicting
verdicts, or a \texttt{Maybe} stays outstanding and is carried forward to the next
adjudication round rather than being resolved by a tie-break, so no final label rests on a
lone human reading. Human verdicts are also sticky: once a row has settled this way it is
not reopened by later changes to the flag rule or by regeneration of the queue, and only a
fresh pair of agreeing verdicts can supersede it. At the point of analysis every flagged
row had settled under this bar: none remained outstanding, and no \texttt{Maybe} survives
in the final labels. All settled judgments are written to a persistent store keyed by
(category, CVE). When a difference set is later regenerated after a term-list change,
prior judgments are restored automatically and only genuinely new rows are re-reviewed,
so no settled work is repeated.

Across all 8{,}124 reviewed (category, CVE) rows, 3{,}753 (46.2\%) settled as true
matches and 4{,}371 (53.8\%) as false positives. This confirms that a text-based search
over NVD carries a substantial false-positive load, one that per-category review has to
remove: on this corpus the majority of candidate rows are noise. The false-positive rate
varies widely by category, and it tracks how distinctive each category's device language
and brand set are. Two categories sit at 100\% for reasons of scope rather than term
collision and we set them aside here, returning to them below: \texttt{shades} (5 of 5),
which has essentially no NVD footprint at all, and \texttt{sleeptracker} (29 of 29).
Among the rest, the rate is highest where a generic brand or device noun collides with
enterprise or unrelated products (air conditioner 96.2\%, thermostat 80.0\%, baby monitor
78.0\%, cameras 77.6\% over 3{,}950 rows), and lowest for categories with unambiguous
device vocabulary and distinctive brand names (home power 13.5\%, garden 29.6\%, hubs
30.0\%). The camera and baby-monitor rates in particular come from an over-broad vendor
list that pulls in professional and enterprise surveillance gear alongside generic IP
cameras; the term-precision pass (Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}) is what turns those
recurring false positives into concrete list-pruning actions. Two categories are
instructive edge cases. The \texttt{sleeptracker} rate, set aside above, is 100\% (29 of 29): its term list
surfaced almost only wrist-worn fitness wearables, which fail the residential-deployment
criterion, and no bedside monitors at all, so the category contributes nothing to the
analysis set. And \texttt{smartspeakers}, at 62.0\%, is not the low-false-positive
category it appears to be by name: a single over-broad term, \texttt{smart display},
accounts for 48 rejected rows on its own, which is precisely the kind of recurring failure
the term-precision loop converts into a prune action rather than a repeated adjudication.
The \texttt{streaming} rate (14.3\% over 2{,}440 rows) should be read alongside the tvOS
scope exclusion of Section~\ref{sec:method-collect}: the review found most of those rows to
be genuine matches, and it is the subsequent scope decision, not the false-positive review,
that removes them. The three AI reviewers agreed pairwise on 87.0\%
(Claude--Codex), 86.3\% (Claude--Gemini), and 85.2\% (Codex--Gemini) of rows, and all
three were unanimous on 80.3\%. The final labels break down as 6{,}467 AI-consensus,
1{,}638 human-adjudicated (human verdicts are sticky across queue regenerations), and 19
strong-consensus, with no rows outstanding.

\subsection{Term-level precision and list refinement}
\label{sec:method-precision}

The matched-term attribution recorded at collection lets us close the quality loop
from the precision side as well. Joining each settled final judgment back to the term(s)
that pulled its CVE into the corpus, we compute a per-term precision for every brand and
keyword term, defined as the fraction of a term's judged difference-set matches that were
confirmed true. We flag as prune candidates any term whose difference-set precision falls
at or below $0.10$ over at least 5 judged rows. In practice this turns a brand string that
collides with unrelated software, or an over-broad vendor entry that pulls in generic
hardware, into a concrete line item to remove from the term list rather than a recurring
disagreement to re-adjudicate. Precision is computed per (term, category, search method),
since the same string can be registered for more than one category or on both term lists;
of the 426 such combinations with at least one judged difference-set match, spanning 398
distinct term strings, 20 crossed this threshold, over 19 distinct terms. Most are vendor terms naming
professional or enterprise product lines (e.g.\ \texttt{synology surveillance station},
\texttt{geutebruck}, and \texttt{digital watchdog} under cameras, each at $0.0$ precision
over 7--25 judged rows), plus a handful of broad consumer-brand entries that dominate
their category's false positives (e.g.\ \texttt{d-link} under cameras at $0.044$ over
1{,}802 judged rows). Five, however, are device-keyword rather than brand terms
(\texttt{smart display}, \texttt{home hub}, \texttt{evse}, \texttt{charge point}, and
\texttt{weather station}), each an umbrella phrase broad enough to match marketing copy
rather than a device. One term, \texttt{smart display}, is registered on \emph{both} lists
and is therefore flagged twice, which is why the 20 flagged combinations span 19 distinct
terms. That the same threshold prunes both lists matters: the loop is not
merely a brand-list filter but a symmetric check on either search method's terms, and the
keyword list is not immune to the over-breadth problem simply because it avoids brand
names. Since these labels cover only the difference set, this is a
prioritized prune list, i.e.\ per-term precision \emph{on the difference set}, not a
global precision over all of a term's matches.

This pass and the automated discovery of Section~\ref{sec:method-collect} are the two
halves of one loop, and they are deliberately asymmetric in what they automate. Discovery
proposes terms a hand-compiled list is missing and requires a human to accept each one,
because accepting a brand is a scope judgment. Pruning identifies terms that have already
been shown to generate noise, using labels the review produced, so the evidence for
removal is measured rather than predicted. Both write only candidate lists, and both are
run between pipeline passes rather than inside one: a term list that changed while a review
was in progress would leave a category's judgments spanning two different search
definitions.

\section{RQ1: Analysis of Vulnerability Classes in Home IoT Devices}
\label{sec:rq1}

To answer RQ1, we refer to Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-matrix}, which maps every
CWE attributed to a confirmed home IoT device CVE onto the CWE-888 primary
vulnerability classes (Section~\ref{sec:method-cwe888}). The table lists the 22
classes reached by at least one CWE in our mapping, of which 21 carry attributions;
the \texttt{Malware} column is retained but empty. Results are broken down by
category. The corpus behind it is 1{,}738 confirmed
CVE--category assignments over 1{,}676 distinct CVEs, yielding 1{,}904 mapped
CWE attributions across the 22 categories with confirmed data.
Figures~\ref{fig:cwe888-treemap-all}--\ref{fig:cwe888-treemap-evcharging}
show the same distribution as area-proportional treemaps — one overall and
one each for the four categories with the most confirmed CVEs (\texttt{cameras},
\texttt{hub}, \texttt{alarms}, \texttt{ev-charging}, the only categories above
$N=75$) — mirroring Figs.~2--4 of the transportation IoT study. Unlike
Table~III of the transportation IoT study, which lists 23 classes as rows
against 4 device categories as columns, this study's categories are shown as
rows against the CWE-888 classes as columns, so that each category's own
dominant classes read across a single line; the shaded cells in each row
(\colorbox{yellow}{\phantom{0}}) mark that category's 6 most common classes,
the same highlighting convention the transportation study uses to identify
its dominant classes per column.

Two findings stand out at the corpus level. First, a small number of classes
account for most of the corpus: \texttt{Tainted Input} (23\%) and
\texttt{Memory Access} (22\%) together cover almost half of all attributions,
followed by \texttt{Authentication} (14\%) and \texttt{Information Leak}
(10\%); the six most common classes account for 81\% of every attribution we
observe. This is a markedly input-validation- and memory-centric profile,
consistent with a device population built largely on embedded C firmware
exposed through network-facing parsers and web interfaces. Second, the
classes that \emph{recur} are not quite the ones that \emph{dominate}.
\texttt{Information Leak} appears in 21 of the 22 categories and
\texttt{Tainted Input} in 19, while \texttt{Memory Access} --- the second
largest class by volume --- appears in only 14. Memory-safety defects are
therefore concentrated rather than universal: they are the signature of
specific device classes, not a property of home IoT as a whole.

That concentration is visible in the per-category rows. \texttt{Memory
Access} accounts for 61\% of \texttt{hub} attributions, 39\% of
\texttt{smartspeakers}, and 36\% of \texttt{streaming} --- the three
categories whose devices run the most substantial general-purpose software
stacks --- against 17\% for \texttt{cameras}. The camera-style categories
instead lead with \texttt{Tainted Input} (29\% for \texttt{cameras}, 40\% for
\texttt{alarms}), reflecting the web-management and RTSP/ONVIF interfaces
those devices expose. \texttt{Access Control} peaks in \texttt{doorlock}
(21\%), the one category where the access-control decision \emph{is} the
device's function. Reading the top-6 share as a concentration measure, the
larger categories cluster tightly in the 76--91\% range, with \texttt{pet}
(76\%) the most diffuse and \texttt{hub} (91\%) the most concentrated.

The top-6 figures of 100\% in \texttt{fans}, \texttt{fridge},
\texttt{sensors}, \texttt{airpurifier}, \texttt{appliances}, and
\texttt{airconditioner} are an artifact of small $N$ rather than a finding:
each of those categories rests on five or fewer confirmed CVEs, so six
classes trivially exhaust them, and a cell reading ``\texttt{Channel} 67\%''
in \texttt{sensors} represents two attributions. We therefore do not
interpret those rows individually, and Section~\ref{sec:rq1-cat1} onward
analyzes the folding categories of Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-family}, which
aggregate them to a level where the percentages are supportable.

Because one category (\texttt{cameras}) supplies 52\% of all attributions,
Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-matrix} reports the overall profile under both
weightings. The micro row pools every attribution, so it describes the
average \emph{vulnerability}; the macro row averages the 22 per-category
profiles with equal weight, so it describes the average \emph{category}. The
two agree on the leading class and on \texttt{Authentication} at 14\%, and
diverge most on \texttt{Memory Access} (22.0\% micro against 14.7\% macro, a
7.3-point gap) and \texttt{Information Leak} (10.4\% against 16.2\%). The
direction of both gaps restates the concentration finding above: memory
defects are over-represented in the largest categories and information
leakage is spread more evenly across small ones. We report the gap as a
robustness check rather than a result --- at 7.3 points it is modest, and it
narrows to 5.3 points once categories are folded
(Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-family}), which is the expected effect of pooling
small categories into better-populated ones.

The subsections that follow discuss the four largest folding categories of
Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-family}, which between them carry 1{,}558 of the 1{,}904
attributions (82\%). The folding categories are the coarser grouping defined
over the 24 leaf categories in the device ontology
(Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}); we discuss results at this level because six
leaf categories rest on five or fewer confirmed CVEs and cannot support
per-category interpretation, while the folded groups are populated enough to
compare. Per-category numbers remain in Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-matrix}
throughout, and the folding never re-labels a CVE --- it is a view over the
same judgments.

\subsection{Cameras and Monitors}
\label{sec:rq1-cat1}

The \texttt{cameras}, \texttt{doorbell}, and \texttt{babymonitor} categories
fold into a single group of 948 confirmed CVEs and 1{,}052 CWE attributions,
55\% of the corpus and by a wide margin the largest group in the study. Its
profile is led by \texttt{Tainted Input} (28\%), followed by \texttt{Memory
Access} (16\%), \texttt{Authentication} (15\%), and \texttt{Information Leak}
(10\%), with a top-6 share of 83\%.

The dominance of \texttt{Tainted Input} is the clearest device-level signal in
our data. These are network-facing devices whose management surface is
typically an embedded web interface and whose media path speaks RTSP or ONVIF,
so they expose an unusually large amount of externally supplied input to
firmware parsers written in C. That the same profile holds across all three
leaf categories --- 29\% for \texttt{cameras}, 21\% for \texttt{doorbell},
18\% for \texttt{babymonitor} --- supports the ontology's decision to fold
them: consumers treat them as three products with distinct brand sets, but
their vulnerability profiles are close enough that the group behaves as one
population. \texttt{Authentication} is the second consistent thread, at
15--23\% across the three, reflecting the recurring pattern of default
credentials and weak session handling in consumer camera firmware. We note
that \texttt{babymonitor} is the category most affected by the vendor-list
contamination discussed in Section~\ref{sec:threats-internal}, so its 22
attributions should be read as the most provisional of the three.

\subsection{Hubs and Controllers}
\label{sec:rq1-cat2}

The \texttt{hub} category stands alone as a folding category, with 247
confirmed CVEs and 282 attributions (15\% of the corpus). Its profile is the
most distinctive in the study: \texttt{Memory Access} alone accounts for 61\%
of its attributions, more than any other class in any other group, and its
top-6 share of 91\% is the highest of the four groups discussed here.

This is a genuine architectural difference rather than a sampling artifact.
Hubs and controllers run the most substantial software stacks of any device
class in scope --- full Linux systems bridging Zigbee, Z-Wave, Thread, and IP,
often with a local automation runtime --- and that additional native code
surface is where memory-safety defects accumulate. The contrast with the
camera group is the sharpest result in Table~\ref{tab:cwe888-family}:
\texttt{Memory Access} is 61\% here against 16\% there, while
\texttt{Tainted Input} inverts, at 10\% here against 28\% there. Cameras are
compromised through what they parse; hubs are compromised through how they are
built. The security implication also differs in kind, because a hub is by
definition a control surface for other devices, so a memory-safety defect
there is a pivot into every device it manages rather than a compromise of one
endpoint.

\subsection{Energy}
\label{sec:rq1-cat3}

The \texttt{ev-charging} and \texttt{home-power} categories fold into an
energy group of 116 confirmed CVEs and 135 attributions (7\%). The ontology
keeps these two leaves separate --- they share no confirmed CVE and their
vendor sets are disjoint --- but folds them for reporting, and their combined
profile is the most evenly spread of the four groups: \texttt{Tainted Input}
(19\%), \texttt{Authentication} (16\%), \texttt{Information Leak} (13\%), and
\texttt{Memory Access} (12\%), with the lowest top-6 concentration of the
group at 78\%.

That flatness is itself the finding. Where cameras and hubs each have a single
defect class accounting for a quarter to two-thirds of their attributions,
energy devices distribute across the full range, which is consistent with a
category that spans two quite different architectures: vehicle-charging
equipment with OCPP-style backend connectivity on one side, and inverters and
home battery systems with cloud-linked monitoring on the other. The elevated
\texttt{Authentication} share (16\%, against 14\% corpus-wide) is notable
given that these devices mediate physical electrical load, where an
authentication bypass has consequences beyond information disclosure.

\subsection{Alarms and Sensors}
\label{sec:rq1-cat4}

The \texttt{alarms} and \texttt{sensors} categories fold into a group of 93
confirmed CVEs and 89 attributions (5\%), though the group is effectively the
\texttt{alarms} category: \texttt{sensors} contributes only 3 attributions and
is one of the six under-populated leaves noted above.

The group's profile is the most \texttt{Tainted Input}-heavy in the study at
38\%, ahead even of the camera group, and it is the only group in which
\texttt{Channel} appears among the leading classes (16\%, against 2\%
corpus-wide). That \texttt{Channel} share is the substantive result here: it
covers defects in the communication path itself, and it surfaces in alarm
systems because they rely on wireless sensor links --- frequently
proprietary sub-GHz protocols --- whose confidentiality and integrity
properties have historically been weak. An alarm system is also the category
where a channel-level defect maps most directly onto the threat the device
exists to counter, since suppressing or spoofing a sensor link defeats the
system without touching its software at all.

\section{RQ2: Analysis of CVSS Scores and Severity}
\label{sec:rq2}

In this section we analyze the CVSS scores and severity levels of the confirmed
home IoT device CVEs, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:cvss-summary}. The CVSS score
ranges from 0 to 10 and is a composite metric that integrates the exploitability
and impact of a vulnerability~\cite{ref-cvss}. Severity is derived from the score
using the standard qualitative bands described in Section~\ref{sec:method-extract}.
Every CVE in the analysis set carries a base score, so no category loses rows to
missing data here.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:cvss-summary}, among the 17 categories with at least
five scored CVEs the highest median CVSS score is found in \texttt{hub}
(median~9.8, mean~8.73, $n=247$), followed by \texttt{alarms} (8.8, 8.06, $n=89$)
and a tie at 8.1 between \texttt{garden} and \texttt{pet}. The lowest medians are
\texttt{airconditioner} (6.8, 7.24, $n=5$) and \texttt{doorlock} (6.95, 7.16,
$n=36$); \texttt{robotvacuum} has the lowest mean of any tested category (6.57)
despite a median of 7.4. We conducted a Kruskal--Wallis test~\cite{kruskal1952} across
these 17 categories and obtained $H=169.820$ ($df=16$), $p=9.1\times10^{-28}$, rejecting
the null hypothesis that there is no difference among the categories' CVSS
scores. Five categories --- \texttt{fans}, \texttt{fridge}, \texttt{sensors},
\texttt{airpurifier}, and \texttt{appliances} --- have fewer than five scored CVEs
($n \le 4$ each) and were excluded from the test; they are reported descriptively
in Table~\ref{tab:cvss-summary} (marked~$\dagger$), but no statistical conclusion
should be drawn from them individually.

The results of Dunn's post-hoc test~\cite{dunn1964} (rank-based, tie-corrected, two-sided,
Bonferroni-adjusted across all $\binom{17}{2}=136$ pairwise comparisons) are
dominated by a single category: 13 pairs are significant after correction, and
\emph{12 of them involve} \texttt{hub}, in every case with \texttt{hub} scoring
higher. The strongest are \texttt{hub} versus \texttt{cameras}
($p_{\text{bonf}}=1.5\times10^{-22}$), \texttt{ev-charging}
($p_{\text{bonf}}=2.8\times10^{-12}$), \texttt{streaming}
($p_{\text{bonf}}=3.7\times10^{-11}$), and \texttt{robotvacuum}
($p_{\text{bonf}}=1.8\times10^{-9}$); \texttt{hub} also scores significantly
higher than \texttt{alarms} ($p_{\text{bonf}}=6.7\times10^{-3}$), the
second-ranked category. The single significant pair not involving \texttt{hub} is
\texttt{alarms} versus \texttt{robotvacuum} ($p_{\text{bonf}}=0.010$). The full
136-pair table is available in the accompanying data release (see the
Data Availability statement).

\texttt{hub} therefore stands out on every measure: the highest median and mean,
and a Critical-severity share of 53.4\% (132 of 247 CVEs), meaning most hub
vulnerabilities in our corpus are rated Critical rather than merely High. This is
consistent with the hub's structural position --- it holds credentials for, and
issues commands to, every device bound to it, so a hub compromise escalates into
control of the devices behind it. \texttt{alarms} shows the same pattern less
sharply (41.6\% Critical), which fits devices whose entire purpose is to resist
an adversary. At the other end, \texttt{lighting} has the highest concentration
in the High band of any category (67.6\%) alongside one of the lowest Critical
shares (5.9\%), and \texttt{ev-charging} and \texttt{doorlock} sit at 11.3\% and
11.1\% Critical respectively.

\emph{This finding carries an important caveat.} The \texttt{hub} category is
heavily concentrated in a single vendor: 111 of its 247 confirmed CVEs (44.9\%)
are attributed to Insteon products, with Samsung SmartThings a distant second at
16.2\%. The category's severity profile therefore substantially reflects one
vendor's disclosure history rather than an established property of home hubs as a
device class, and the Dunn comparisons involving \texttt{hub} should be read with
that concentration in mind. We report the result because it is what the corpus
shows, but a replication on a hub population with a broader vendor base is needed
before treating hub severity dominance as a general finding.

A second caveat concerns score comparability. The distribution pools CVSS v2.0,
v3.x, and v4.0 base scores (Section~\ref{sec:method-extract}), and v2.0 uses a
different formula that skews low, making it a confound rather than mere noise. As
a sensitivity check we re-ran the analysis excluding the 81 CVEs scored only under
v2.0, leaving 1{,}657 scored CVEs. The omnibus result is unchanged in direction
and slightly stronger ($H=181.181$, $p=4.9\times10^{-30}$, same 17 categories),
so the conclusions above do not rest on the pooling decision.

Unlike the transportation IoT study, which manually extracted the most
vulnerable \emph{components} (e.g.\ web interface, RKE system, infotainment
system) from each CVE description and cross-tabulated them against severity (its
Table~IV), this study has not yet performed an analogous component-level
extraction. \stub[A component-level breakdown, mirroring Table~IV of the
transportation study, is left as future work (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}) and
would let us test whether specific attack surfaces --- e.g.\ companion mobile
apps, cloud APIs, or physical Zigbee/BLE radio stacks --- concentrate the
Critical/High-severity vulnerabilities the way RKE and web-interface components
did in that study.]

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Construct Validity}
\label{sec:threats-construct}

Our operationalization of ``home IoT device'' rests on five definitional criteria
(Section~\ref{sec:method-scope}), and those criteria require judgment calls at the
margin: whether a streaming device's platform genuinely acts as a home-control surface,
say, or whether a brand's product line is residential rather than commercial or
industrial. We mitigated this by freezing the category list before large-scale collection
and by pinning a shared, written scope note per category (\texttt{categories.csv})
that every reviewer, human or AI, judges against. Even so, the underlying category
boundaries are themselves a modeling choice, and a different set of criteria could redraw
them.

Two further checks bound this threat. First, encoding the five criteria as OWL axioms
(Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}) lets us test whether our published scope decisions
actually follow from our stated criteria, rather than from case-by-case intuition. An
OWL~2~RL reasoner over the criterion facets reproduces our in-scope and out-of-scope
rulings, so no category is in the study for reasons its own criteria do not support. On
its own that is a weak result, because most of those rulings are in-scope categories that
the axiom admits by construction. The check with real force is the deletion test of
Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}: removing any one of the five criteria from the membership
axiom causes the reasoner to object, because a defined-but-excluded boundary type fails on
that criterion alone. All five criteria are load-bearing under this test, so none is
decorative. Both remain consistency checks rather than validity proofs --- the facet values
are themselves assigned by us, so the reasoner confirms that our decisions are internally
coherent, not that the criteria are the right ones. Second, aligning the categories to established
smart-home vocabularies quantifies how far the scope departs from existing practice. The
alignment procedure and the vocabularies searched are described in
Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}. Of the 24 categories, 7 (29\%) have an
exactly corresponding external class; the remaining 17 (71\%) can be denoted only by a
coarser one.

Two distinct gaps sit inside that figure, and the second is the more consequential. Only
a generic superclass --- \texttt{saref:Device}, \texttt{saref:Appliance},
\texttt{saref:Sensor} or \texttt{saref:Actuator} --- denotes 6 of the 24 categories
(25\%): door locks, EV charging, pet devices, robot vacuums, refrigerators and air
purifiers. These are small categories, carrying 10.0\% of our confirmed CVEs. The larger
problem is granularity rather than absence. A single class,
\texttt{s4bldg:AudioVisualAppliance}, denotes five of our categories --- cameras, baby
monitors, doorbells, smart speakers and streaming devices --- which together carry 61.1\%
of our confirmed CVEs. Device types with distinct brand sets, threat models and CVE
profiles collapse into one class, and the home-control role by which streaming devices and
smart speakers enter our scope at all is not expressible in these vocabularies. This
supports the choice to define categories from consumer product boundaries rather than
adopt an existing vocabulary, but it is equally a limitation on comparability: our
categories cannot be mapped one-to-one onto studies built on those vocabularies. We note
one limit of the manifest check itself: it verifies that the IRIs we cite exist, and can
never detect a class we failed to look for --- an earlier version of this analysis stopped
at SAREF core and consequently overstated the absence of denoting classes.

Mapping individual CWEs onto the CWE-888 Software Fault Pattern clusters
(Section~\ref{sec:method-cwe888}) is a second construct-validity threat. When a CWE is not
itself in CWE-888, we map through its parent CWEs, and a CWE whose parents span two
primary clusters is counted in both, which can inflate cluster totals relative to a
stricter one-CWE-one-cluster scheme. We follow the same mapping convention as prior
work~\cite{ref-mcsoftware} to keep the two studies comparable, but both the choice of view
(CWE-888 over, e.g., CWE-699) and the parent-resolution rule shape the resulting ``big
picture'' of vulnerability classes.

\subsection{Internal Validity}
\label{sec:threats-internal}

The vendor and keyword searches are text-based over CVE descriptions and CPE strings,
so classification errors are possible in either direction. A generic brand name can
collide with an unrelated product (the per-term precision analysis,
Section~\ref{sec:method-precision}, found 20 of 426 judged term--category--method
combinations at or below the
0.10-precision prune threshold, e.g.\ vendor terms that matched only unrelated enterprise
products in every judged row), and a device-relevant CVE can contain none of our search
terms at all. We addressed the first failure mode with the three-AI-plus-human review of
every difference-set row and with the term-precision pass that flags terms for pruning.
We addressed the second only partially, through the CPE-expansion step
(Section~\ref{sec:method-collect}), which finds additional CVEs attributed to
already-confirmed device products independent of description wording. The review is
complete: all 8{,}124 reviewed rows carry a settled label, with none
outstanding. A residual error rate nonetheless remains even on the rows that never
reached a human. We validated the strong-consensus relaxation at 99.7\% human agreement
(293 of 294 rows), so on the order of one in a few hundred high-confidence AI-consensus
rows may still be misclassified, and that residual is concentrated in exactly the
high-false-positive categories (cameras, baby monitor, air conditioner) where the
reviewers face the hardest calls. Reviewer disagreement is itself a threat. Codex is
systematically more conservative, rejecting unfamiliar security-focused brands, while
Gemini/Gemma is systematically more liberal, accepting function-overlap across categories
(e.g.\ IP camera $\to$ baby monitor). For that reason we treat Gemini as the weaker third
vote and exclude its confidence from the human-review flag; its judgment still counts
toward the unanimity check, however, so residual bias from either model can still shape
which rows are, and are not, escalated to a human.

\subsection{External Validity}
\label{sec:threats-external}

Our dataset is bounded by NVD's CVE disclosure and CPE-tagging practices, not by actual
real-world deployment or exploitation. A device with no publicly disclosed CVE is
invisible to this study, however insecure it may be in practice, and smaller or newer
vendors are structurally under-represented relative to established brands with active CVE
reporting histories and extensive CPE tagging. CPE data is also known to lag on recent
(2024--2026) CVEs (Section~\ref{sec:method-review}), so the most recent portion of the
corpus is more likely to be classified from description text alone. The snapshot is a
single fixed point in time (2026-08-05), so any vulnerability disclosed after that date is
out of scope by construction, and the study reports a static ``as-of'' picture rather than
a continuously updated one. Finally, since both search methods are text-based, a
device-relevant CVE whose description and CPE strings contain none of our vendor or
keyword terms never enters the dataset, and the CPE-expansion step reaches only products
already confirmed in scope. The analysis therefore characterizes the CVEs our terms
surfaced for each category, not every CVE that may affect it.

\subsection{Reliability}
\label{sec:threats-reliability}

To support reproducibility, all searches run against one fixed, dated, and documented
NVD snapshot (Section~\ref{sec:method-collect}) rather than the live NVD API, so the
corpus a reader would reconstruct today matches the one analyzed here. Every
difference-set judgment, human or AI, is persisted in a store keyed by (category, CVE),
so the review can be audited, re-run, or extended without silently losing or duplicating
prior work. The one part of the pipeline that is not fully deterministic is the
Gemini/Gemma reviewer, whose free-tier model (\texttt{gemma-4-31b-it}) may be swapped or
updated over time. We mitigate this by keeping one model fixed across an entire review
pass and recording which model produced each Gemini judgment.

Reliability of the \emph{ontology facets} is a separate question from reliability of the
CVE judgments, and we measured it directly rather than assuming it. Three independent
annotators labelled a shared subsample of sampled devices, yielding 471 facet--item
judgments over 35--40 devices per facet, and we report Fleiss' $\kappa$~\cite{fleiss1971}
with bootstrap
confidence intervals (Table~\ref{tab:facet-kappa}). Only 4 of 12 facets clear the
conventional $\kappa \geq 0.60$ bar. We therefore treat the facet vocabulary as
\emph{organizing structure} rather than as evidence: facets group categories and generate
hypotheses, and no finding in this paper rests on a facet as its grouping variable. All
496 facet assertions in the published ontology are recorded as \texttt{Estimated}
provenance, and the build gate prints that mix on every run so it cannot fade from view.

Three results here are worth stating explicitly, because each bounds a claim we might
otherwise have made. First, \texttt{supportLifetime} scores \emph{below chance}
($\kappa = -0.311$, 12.5\% raw agreement, 28.3\% ``unsure''). That is not a difficult
facet but a broken instrument: annotators are either reading the value definitions
differently or answering a question the input cannot support, namely a vendor's support
policy inferred from a product name. More annotation would not repair it. Second,
\texttt{hasWebAdminUI} (0.349) and \texttt{computeTier} (0.384) sit at the bottom of the
table, and they are precisely the two facets an independent attempt to \emph{derive}
facet values from CVE description text also failed on. Text derivation and human
annotation share no mechanism, so their agreement about which facets are unassignable is
considerably stronger evidence than either result alone. This is also why we report no
contrast grouped by web-admin presence: the derivation attempt found the apparent effect
size to be governed by the author's choice of keyword list, with 19 of 22 categories
swinging by more than 25 points between a narrow and a loose word list. Third,
\texttt{capturesAV}~--- the facet at the center of our device-heterogeneity
correction~--- fails reliability ($\kappa = 0.386$) as well as validity, so we report it
only as a distribution and never as a category-level value.

Reliability presupposes validity, and the two can fail independently: three annotators
can agree perfectly on a value that is false of the devices it is stamped onto. Because
our facets are asserted per \emph{category} but joined onto \emph{CVE} rows, a category
holding two device subfamilies has a facet value that is a fiction on part of its rows.
We measured this separately by sampling devices within each category (272 devices across
the 10 categories with enough CVE mass to sample) and computing each cell's modal share
(Section~\ref{sec:method-ontology}). Of the 120 measured cells, 86 are defensible as a
single category-level value, 22 are usable only for grouping, and 12 are not usable at
all. Cells failing that
gate are withheld from analysis \emph{in code} rather than by policy note, and a second
withholding route catches cells where two reviewers split along a subfamily line in
categories too small to sample at all. This is why \texttt{capturesAV} appears here as a
withheld distribution rather than as a headline number.

We do not report a facet-level triage of human reviewer disagreement, which an earlier
plan for this work proposed. Attributing each two-reviewer split to the facet that drove
it would require using facets as the grouping variable for a reliability analysis, and
the $\kappa$ panel above establishes that most of them are not reliable enough to carry
that weight. Reporting such a triage would invert the evidentiary order, explaining a
measured disagreement in terms of an unmeasured one.

\section*{Data Availability}
\label{sec:conclusion:data-availability}

The scripts and derived datasets supporting this study are available at
\url{https://github.com/constrictingsnake/Home-IoT-Security}.
% RELEASE GATE: before the camera-ready, remove Onboarding-Docs/ (third-party copyrighted
% PDFs) from the public artifact, and decide whether Design.pdf / "Devices List.docx" ship.
% Consider minting a DOI (e.g. via Zenodo) and citing that instead of the bare Git URL.
The repository includes: the vendor/brand and keyword term
lists used for collection (\texttt{vendor\_terms.csv}, \texttt{keyword\_terms.csv}); the
raw per-category search outputs (\texttt{results\_all\_<category>.csv},
\texttt{keyword\_<category>.csv}); the offline search engine and pipeline scripts (CVE
collection, set reconciliation, CPE expansion, review generation and merging, and
term-precision); the shared classification rubric and per-category scope notes used
by every reviewer (\texttt{CLASSIFICATION\_PROMPT.md}, \texttt{categories.csv}); the
blind per-reviewer judgment files and their merged, human-adjudicated, and finalized outputs
for every category; and the persistent judgment store keyed by (category, CVE) that backs
the entire review process. The device ontology is included as a citable artifact in its own
right: \texttt{homeiot.ttl} (the OWL~2 class hierarchy, criterion facets, and folding
categories), \texttt{shapes.ttl} (the SHACL constraints every class must satisfy),
\texttt{homeiot-align.ttl} (alignment to SAREF and SSN/SOSA, kept in a separate file so it
can be dropped without affecting the pipeline, together with the pinned manifest of
external class IRIs it is validated against), and \texttt{families.csv} (the generated
leaf-to-folding-category map used for the rollups in Section~\ref{sec:rq1}). We also
publish \texttt{homeiot-kg.ttl}, an RDF knowledge graph of the confirmed dataset that
expresses every CVE, product, vendor, category assignment, and CWE-888 class as linked
data, so the counts reported in this paper can be reproduced by SPARQL query rather than
by re-running the pipeline. The one artifact not tracked in the repository is the bulk NVD
snapshot itself (\texttt{nvd\_all.csv}, 373{,}518 CVE records merged from the NVD 2.0
API as of 2026-08-05), which is excluded for size rather than for access reasons; the
snapshot's provenance and exact rebuild procedure are documented in
\texttt{data/nvd-snapshot/SNAPSHOT.md} so that it can be regenerated in full from public NVD
data. The repository is public and carries no access restrictions. Rebuilding the snapshot
requires a free NVD API key, and re-running the automated third reviewer requires a Gemini
API key; both are read from a local, gitignored \texttt{.env} file and are not themselves
part of the dataset, so every artifact needed to reproduce the analysis from the published
derived data is included without credentials.
